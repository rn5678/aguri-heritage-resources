Reading the source labels

Original Bengali journal identifies a community publication. Colonial ethnography records an outside classification in its historical setting. Scholarship identifies an attributed research work.

The labels describe the kind of evidence and our access to it. They are not a simple ranking of truth. An original publication can contain argument, error, aspiration or prejudice; identifying the author’s standpoint remains part of the work.

MASAHIKO TOGAWA · 2006

An Abode of the Goddess

Read this to explore how a temple’s ritual year connected families, service land and village authority in Kshiragram.

Reading scope and access

An anthropological study of Kshiragram and its Jogadya temple, based on fieldwork in 1994–95 and first written as a 1996 University of North Bengal thesis. It records the nine Ugra Kshatriya lineages, their service land from the Bardhaman Maharaja, their places in the temple’s ritual year, their lineage Durga Pujas and a Raychaudhuri family chart.[cite:togawa-2006]

A single village seen in one period, but the closest view yet of how Aguri lineages, Brahman priests, Bagdi servants and the Raj fitted together.

Historical scholarship

John R. McLane, Land and Local Kingship in Eighteenth-Century Bengal (1993)

Cambridge University Press, Cambridge South Asian Studies 53. Village “oligarchies” of dominant peasants, “typically Aguris, Kaibartas, or Sadgops” (pp. 54–55); “the raja of Burdwan’s naqdi or cash-paid army”, whose salaries fell from about Rs 4 lakh to just over Rs 1 lakh in 1761–67, and the 1760s table of 32,032 land-paid servants (pp. 117–118); chapter 6, “Mughal Burdwan and the rise of the Burdwan raj” (pp. 125–138): Burdwan as a Mughal garrison town, the Khatri origins of the Raj family, Abu Rai’s appointment (p. 134), Krishnaram Rai’s farman of 1695–96 (p. 137) and the revolt of 1696–97 (p. 129); Gopbhum, Oldham’s Khatri–Sadgop theory and the Aguris (pp. 157–160); the Raja’s unpaid troops and the battle of 29 December 1760 (pp. 183–185); Johnstone’s payroll cut and the 1767 reduction of the “naqdi or household guard” from 1,487 to 839 (p. 192); Kinloch’s 1787 halving of the zamindari troops to 1,000 (p. 263); 17,384 village paiks in 1794 (p. 271). Printed pp. 125–126 describe Burdwan’s embankments, flood damage and cultivators cutting banks for irrigation.

Access and scope

Read directly from the full Cambridge PDF (October 2026): pp. 54–55, 117–118, 125–138, 157–160, 183–185, 192, 263 and 271. McLane uses the Chandimangal, dated 1589 and quoted through Dimock and Inden’s translation, to treat the Aguris as older than the Khatris in Burdwan; this website records that reading and its limits on the Origins page. McLane does not connect the Aguris with the Raj’s naqdi (cash-paid) army or any other troops.
