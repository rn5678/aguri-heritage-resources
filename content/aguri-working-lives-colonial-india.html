Bijaygobinda Chaudhuri did not remain in the first government job he accepted. A scholarship pupil who had begun studying beyond school, he entered the Jail Department as a deputy jailer. According to his family history, he disliked the work and moved to the Registration Department as a sub-registrar. His subsequent postings took him through Bengal and Bihar. After retirement, he became president of the high-school committee at Putsuri. [1]

The change of occupation gives a small, revealing glimpse of a life usually compressed into a designation. It also offers a way into the history of Aguris, or Ugra Kshatriyas, in colonial public service. Their working lives included choices, interrupted studies and responsibilities to relatives. Some careers led far from the family home; others eventually brought their holders back to village schools or local public affairs.

These stories survive in very different places. An official civil list supplies an appointment and a date. A Bengali family history remembers the relative who provided a schoolboy with a home. A community journal records a subscription or the election of a treasurer. Read together, they recover individual lives rooted in Bengal but extending to Bihar, Orissa and the Bombay Presidency. They also let us distinguish employment under the colonial government from elected civic office, private practice and work for a community association.

One family across several departments

Bhuvanmohan Chaudhuri of Putsuri, the second son of Ambikacharan, became a police sub-inspector. His nephew, Rai Bahadur Dolgobinda Chaudhuri, followed a different route: he taught at Berhampore before beginning a prison career that the family historian reckoned at thirty-five years. Dolgobinda was jailer at Puri in 1886 and retired as jailer at Dacca in 1910. The account remembers his final years on a pension, ending at the Purbasthali home of his younger brother Bijaygobinda. [1]

For Bijaygobinda, education had opened the first part of that journey. He won a minor-examination scholarship at Azimganj in 1874 and passed the Entrance examination from Khagra School in 1876. He began F.A. study, the intermediate course preceding a degree, before accepting the deputy-jailer appointment. Registration work and honorary-magistrate duties were followed, in retirement, by responsibility for the Putsuri high-school committee. [1]

His son Siddheswar Chaudhuri, born on 27 June 1893, took a longer route through higher education. The family account records English honours at Presidency College, a Dwarkanath scholarship, an M.A. and a law qualification, followed by teaching at Burdwan School and Scottish Church College. The Bengal Civil List independently dates his entry into government service to 6 February 1918. [1][2]

By 1932, Siddheswar was prosecutor in excise, salt and opium cases in Calcutta and the districts, with public-prosecutor powers. The official record gives substance to the family biography’s account of a legal and administrative career. His public role widened with service in the Bengal Legislative Council in 1932–33, where the Council roll lists him as an expert nominated member. [1][2]

These careers unfolded within colonial institutions of policing, confinement and revenue collection. The family account also follows the men beyond their appointments, into Dolgobinda’s years on pension and Bijaygobinda’s school work.

An education shared between relatives

In the Hati family, the practical help behind a professional education is especially clear. Kshetramohan Hati learned Persian and Arabic, practised in the Suri Judge’s Court and later became a munsif, a subordinate civil judge. By 1869 he was serving at Kandi, where his grandnephew Banwarilal Hati, later Rai Bahadur, came to study under his care. After retirement, Kshetramohan became dewan of the Jamua Raj in Murshidabad, moving from a government judicial appointment to estate employment. [3]

Banwarilal’s elder brother Biharilal got the highest place and a gold medal in his final Medical College examination in Calcutta in 1872. He began medical work in Howrah the following year, and Banwarilal lived with him while continuing his schooling. A place to stay could be as consequential to an education as an examination result.[3]

Rai Bahadur Banwarilal Hati passed F.A. in 1877 and B.A. in 1879, studied law in 1881–82 and began practice at Berhampore in 1882, following Kshetramohan’s advice. He briefly served as munsif at Pingna in Mymensingh in early 1888. The 1923 biography dates his appointment as Burdwan Public Prosecutor to 1899. He received the title Rai Bahadur in 1917. [3]

The same source records a long commitment to local institutions. Banwarilal was vice-chairman of the Burdwan District Board from 1892 to 1918. At Adhra, his home village, he paid for school and charitable-dispensary buildings and for land required for the Galsi–Adhra road. [3]

His community work is unusually well documented in contemporary print. The minutes of the Ugra Kshatriya Samiti’s sixth meeting, held on 31 July–1 August 1892, record his election as treasurer. The journal also preserves his signature, with others, on a petition to the Bengal Government challenging Risley’s description of the community. Here a professional life connects directly with organized efforts to influence how Aguris were represented. [4]

The school, dispensary and road belonged to the public life of a locality. His treasurership and the petition belonged to the work of a community organization. Banwarilal’s career matters for both, and the distinction makes each contribution clearer.

Surveying and railway employment

Government service was sometimes one stage in a much longer working life. Srinarayan Kundu, the eldest son of the Hooghly pleader Nilmadhab Kundu, worked as a government surveyor. After his father’s death, he resigned to manage the family estates. By 1883 he had a contracting business in the Bombay Presidency, which his son Bijaynarayan Kundu, later Rai Bahadur, joined. Srinarayan died in 1901. [5]

His biography brings together salaried technical work, responsibilities inherited at home and enterprise at a considerable distance from Bengal: government work, estate management and contracting far from Bengal. [5]

Railway employment appears in the Putsuri history in a much shorter entry. Ramdas Chaudhuri, Ambikacharan’s fourth son in the Putsuri family, is explicitly described as a railway employee. The source gives neither his employer nor his rank. It remembers that he died leaving three daughters. That brief entry deserves a place beside the fuller careers of prosecutors and senior officials. [1]

The family account also follows Ramdas’s youngest daughter into her marriage with Radhikaprasad Chaudhuri. It names Radhikaprasad’s eldest son, Bibhutibhushan, as the founder of Deshbandhu Mishtanna Bhandar on Harrison Road in Calcutta. The passage connects this railway employee’s extended family with Calcutta’s commercial life. [1]

An entry in the Ugra Kshatriya Patrika subscriber pages adds a second railway occupation: station master. The personal name is provisionally read as Harendranath Chaudhuri and the address as Darbhanga. The job title is clearer than either reading. The entry places a railway employee among the journal’s readers.[6]

Doctors and the everyday work of public health

Medicine appears within the Putsuri family too. Kapileshwar Chaudhuri, Ambikacharan’s third son, passed F.A., qualified from medical college and entered government employment. The family history records his premature death. It gives no posting or medical-service rank, leaving a short but definite account of an Aguri medical graduate in government work. [1]

A different kind of medical career emerges from the records of Presidency College. An archival survey describes admission forms submitted by Manik Lal Choudhury in 1943 and 1945. They identify his father, Dr K. L. Choudhury, as a mosquito-control officer; the later form specifies Calcutta Corporation. A detail entered for a student’s admission thus preserves the occupation of a municipal public-health official. His initials remain unexpanded. [7]

Medical education could also depend on support from outside the immediate household. Bengali newspaper accounts of the Panja family place Dhanapati Panja’s childhood at Majigram and record help with education from Maharaja Manindra Chandra Nandy. Dhanapati appears as an M.B. doctor in the Calcutta subscriber list around 1931, and later reporting remembers him as a dermatologist of the British period. His son’s September 1947 admission record also describes him as a physician. His medical practice is documented, although no government appointment has been established. [6][7][8]

Doctors also participated in community institutions. In The Social History of a Bengal Town, Indrani Ganguly identifies the medical practitioner Kumar Bihari Samanta among the influential members of the Ugra Kshatriya Samaj in Burdwan. Her account places him among those who chaired local meetings and attended sessions in Calcutta. This gives a specific community role to a practitioner whose fuller professional history is still obscure. [9]

Public work close to home

Local government offers another view of service. The 1934 Pal-family history names Nrisinghamurari Pal, Ganganarayan’s eldest son, as president of the Khano Union Board. Its author praises his efforts to improve the water supply in the union’s villages. [10]

Bimalakinkar Sahana, a paternal first cousin of the writer and zamindar Rai Bahadur Satya Kinkar Sahana, combined business with elected office. Their fathers, Rajkrishna and Prankrishna, were brothers. The 1932 Sahana-family account says that he lived chiefly in Giridih, travelled to Koderma to supervise mica work and was an elected member of Giridih municipality. [11]

In Burdwan, legal practice, local office and community organization also overlapped in the life of Mrityunjoy Chaudhuri. Ganguly names him as an influential Ugra Kshatriya Samaj member and discusses him as municipal vice-chairman in her account of affairs in 1918. His example, like Kumar Bihari’s, shows professional men giving time to the meetings through which community institutions operated. [9]

These forms of work were close to everyday needs: schooling, medical care and water supply. They give public service a local scale, beyond the formal appointments recorded in a civil list.

The wider circle of the community journal

The subscriber pages open a wider professional world, connecting the journal with readers working far from its main centres of circulation. The careers of an irrigation engineer and a clinical pathologist give substance to those brief printed entries. [6]

Rai Sahib Surath Nath Chaudhuri appears at Cuttack in the subscribers. The Bihar and Orissa Civil List for April 1931 independently identifies him as officiating Executive Engineer of the Mahanadi Division in the Irrigation Department. Its honours appendix records a personal Rai Sahib title awarded in 1924. A Bengali family history also records his daughter Rajlakshmi’s marriage to the Calcutta lawyer Bishnupada Pal. [6][10][12]

Dr Gopipada Khan’s working life can be followed through contemporary medical publications. A notice records his temporary appointment as an Assistant Surgeon from 22 June 1916, after graduation from Medical College, Calcutta. In January 1923, his own article names him as Clinical Pathologist at Medical College Hospital, Calcutta, with M.B. and D.P.H. qualifications. [13]

His paper investigated a practical difficulty in testing for typhoid: some cases gave a reaction with a non-motile bacterial strain when the usual strain produced none. It is a rare opportunity to see a subscriber’s actual professional inquiry, rather than only a qualification beside his name. The name, degree, city and period closely connect him with the Gopipada Khan in the journal. [6][13]

Other entries preserve smaller pieces of professional chronology. The historical medical register records Ganganarayan Samanta’s Calcutta University M.B. in 1927 and Bengal registration in 1928, closely matching the Calcutta subscriber. Radhagobinda Hazra of Bora probably corresponds to the registered Radhagobinda Hajra of Boro-Balaram, with an L.M.F. qualification from 1924 and registration in 1927. These are useful additions to the journal’s medical network, with the second identification still provisional. Their hospital or government appointments are not recorded in these entries. [6][14]

Technical education also left traces outside salaried service. The Sahana-family history says that Mrigendranath Samanta, a Raina landholder related to the family by marriage, took the overseer examination at Shibpur Engineering College before managing his property and business. [11]

Remembering the work as well as the name

Bijaygobinda’s school committee and Banwarilal’s contributions at Adhra give us places to return to after the appointments and qualifications. So do a village water supply, a municipal seat and the meetings of a community association. These lives show how professional work could intersect with obligations to relatives and participation in local institutions.

The surviving accounts are uneven. A prosecutor may occupy several pages while a railway employee receives only a line; even a doctor may survive most clearly in his son’s admission papers. Reading those traces together allows a broader Aguri public history to take shape, one attentive to the work people did and to the particular institutions in which they left a record.
