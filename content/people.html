A small community, a wide contribution

In the 1921 census record, Aguris numbered about 69,000, roughly 0.14% of undivided Bengal’s population. Brahmins and Kayasthas each numbered around 13 lakh, approximately 2.7% each.[a] Even in Burdwan, their principal heartland, recorded Aguris formed only about 4.2% of the district’s population that year.[b] From the freedom struggle and land reform to medicine, mathematics, literature, publishing and cinema, the lives gathered here show how this small community helped shape Bengal’s wider public and cultural life.

[b] Dutta (2023), Table 13.1, p. 305: 59,949 Aguris; Thompson (1923), p. 4: Burdwan total 1,438,926. Both refer to 1921.

The collection traces generations of contribution to Bengal’s public life. Figures such as Pratap Chandra Roy and Satya Kinkar Sahana shaped literature and scholarship; Jadabendra Nath Panja, Benoy Choudhury, Hare Krishna Konar and Dasarathi Tah took part in the freedom struggle and later politics. Dr Ganapati Panja and Dr Manohar Ray contributed to medicine, science and education, while Ramapada Chowdhury and Shakti Samanta gained distinction in literature and cinema. Tuhin Kumar Roy contributed to chemical engineering and industrial research, while Krishnadas Paul (K. D. Paul), founder of Bisk Farm, brought experience in trade and distribution to food manufacturing. Together, these lives reflect a broad legacy of service in scholarship, culture, medicine, engineering, enterprise and democratic politics.

Revolutionary prisoner · Peasant movements · Land reform

Hare Krishna Konar

5 August 1915 – 23 July 1974 · Peasant organiser, legislator and land minister[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021]

Hare Krishna Konar was a revolutionary organiser and peasant leader who served as West Bengal’s land and land revenue minister in the United Front governments of 1967 and 1969–70. His work brought rural organising, cultivators’ knowledge and the law together in the struggle over land.[cite:konar][cite:konar-nielsen-land-power]

In 1930, a schoolboy clung to a pillar outside Burdwan’s Congress office while his father tried to take him home. The boy was Hare Krishna Konar. Syed Shahedullah preserved the scene from Benoy Choudhury’s recollections.[cite:konar-shahedullah-remembrance]

Konar’s career joined the struggle for independence to struggles over power in the countryside. He wanted poor cultivators to act together and defend their livelihoods. He carried that concern into government as West Bengal’s land and land revenue minister in the United Front administrations of 1967 and 1969–70.[cite:konar][cite:konar-marxist-2015]

His legislative career began in 1957, a decade after independence. The experience he brought into the Assembly had been acquired largely outside its walls.[cite:konar-kalna-election-record]

The family he came from

Sukanta Konar’s remembrance places his birth on 5 August 1915 in Kamargaria, in Burdwan’s Raina area, in a prosperous family. His father, Sarat Chandra Konar, a landowner who owned a wholesale rice market. He later moved to Memari.[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021]

The economist Ashok Mitra described the family as prosperous and Ugra Kshatriya. This locates Konar within a particular rural social world; it does not, by itself, explain the political choices he made.[cite:konar-ashok-mitra-2014]

His wife was Bibha Konar. According to Shahedullah, she shared his underground life within three months of their marriage, accepting its uncertainties.[cite:konar-shahedullah-remembrance]

Bibha also appears in the history of public work in her own right. A Bengali study of women’s response to the 1943 famine names her among the organisers associated with relief kitchens and milk distribution for children in Burdwan. It places this work within the Mahila Atmaraksha Samiti, the women’s self-defence organisation, and cites Benoy Choudhury’s memoir. Food collection, cooking and distribution helped sustain the wider political movement while meeting immediate needs. Remembering this work gives a fuller picture of the Konar household and of the women who made public action possible.[cite:konar-bibha-famine-relief]

Sukanta Konar’s later remembrance in Deshhitaishee records that gold Hare Krishna had received from his father was sold to sustain a political commune. Family resources were being turned towards collective work.[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021]

Konar’s younger brother, Benoy Krishna Konar, also became a prominent peasant organiser and a legislator from Memari. Benoy’s wife, Maharani Konar, organised among women and herself served in the Assembly. Maharani was Hare Krishna’s sister-in-law.[cite:benoy-bengali][cite:konar-maharani-obituary]

A young revolutionary learns a different politics

Konar joined the Civil Disobedience movement as a school student in 1930. Arrested in 1932 while studying at Bangabasi College, he received a six-year sentence for revolutionary activity. His imprisonment included the Andamans’ Cellular Jail. He was still a teenager when his long confinement began.[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021][cite:konar-zsi-prisoner-list]

He arrived at the Cellular Jail on April 1933 and  took part in a hunger strike begun there on 12 May 1933 against the conditions of the jail. Three prisoners, Mahavir Singh, Mohan Kishore Namadas and Mohit Moitra, died after being force-fed; the strike ended after 46 days, on 26 June, when the authorities conceded demands that included light in the cells, bedding and the right to study. He organised Communist Consolidation among the prisoners in 1935. On 9 July 1937 he and Shiv Verma petitioned the Viceroy, and on 25 July the political prisoners began a second, mass hunger strike. It lasted 36 days and ended after appeals in August from Gandhi, Rabindranath Tagore and the Congress Working Committee; the government then began returning the Andaman prisoners to jails on the mainland. Konar was released on 27 March 1938.[cite:konar-wikipedia]

In prison he encountered Marxist ideas and joined the circle known as Communist Consolidation. He emerged in 1938 and joined the Communist Party. The change gave his anti-colonial convictions a new direction: sustained organisation among workers and peasants, initially in the Kolkata–Howrah area and then especially in Burdwan. The local struggles included opposition to canal taxation and demands for protection from flooding along the Ajay.[cite:konar]

Food and security before independence

During the 1943 famine, Shahedullah recalled Konar organising volunteers preparing and distributing ration cards in Burdwan. His skill with accounts became useful to the work of feeding people.[cite:konar-shahedullah-remembrance]

The joint importance of food and organisation becomes clearer beside the women’s relief work. Community kitchens needed supplies, people to prepare meals and arrangements for reaching children. Such activity created relationships between organisers and households facing catastrophe. It also makes the meaning of service concrete: concern had to become a working arrangement, carried out by many people.[cite:konar-bibha-famine-relief]

For the 1946 election, Shahedullah remembers Konar campaigning for Indrajit Gupta in the coalfields. Attacked near Kajora, he reportedly cycled to Chhora with a fractured leg and stayed until polling ended. His role was election organiser, before he became a legislator.[cite:konar-shahedullah-remembrance]

Independence did not end his experience of repression. After the Communist Party was banned in 1948, he was imprisoned and subsequently worked underground until 1952. He later helped found the CPI(M) in 1964.[cite:konar]

Village grievances enter the Assembly

Konar won election from Kalna in 1957 and again in 1962, 1967, 1969 and 1971. His first two victories were as a CPI candidate; the later three were for the CPI(M), formed after the communist split of 1964. These dates place his parliamentary work firmly in independent India.[cite:konar-kalna-election-record]

On 3 July 1957, he opposed the renewal of West Bengal’s land requisition and acquisition law. In anthropologist Abhijit Guha’s account of the Assembly proceedings, Konar cited standing crops destroyed during acquisition and argued that the same loss of acreage affected a poor cultivator more severely than a wealthy owner. Development, he insisted, should not impoverish the people whose land it required.[cite:konar-guha-acquisition-law]

New Age of 22 March 1959 reported his notice of a bill to amend the 1955 Land Reforms Act. It proposed allowing sharecroppers, or bargadars, to choose where to store or thresh their harvest and to deposit the owner’s share with an official where ownership was disputed. Other proposals protected against eviction, while retaining limited rights of small owners genuinely resuming cultivation, and offered a 50 per cent rent cut to cultivating tenants, with revenue remission after crop failure, flood or drought. The report establishes a proposal, rather than a law already passed.[cite:konar-new-age-bill-1959]

A sharecropper cultivated another person’s land and paid part of the harvest as rent. Control of the threshing floor could therefore affect bargaining power over the crop. The proposal sought to give legal force to that practical concern, while recognising that small owners and powerful landlords did not occupy the same economic position.[cite:konar-new-age-bill-1959]

Later in 1959, Konar joined the Assembly opposition’s criticism of the government over the food movement and its handling of protesters. Sibaji Pratim Basu’s archival study names him among the participating legislators in the debates following the upheaval. Food scarcity, police action and accountability were linked in this confrontation.[cite:konar-food-movement-1959]

His reach also became national. In 1968 he was elected general secretary of the All India Kisan Sabha, an office he held until his death.[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021]

A minister faces the power behind the paperwork

As land minister, Konar confronted a central obstacle: land reform could be frustrated even when legislation already existed. Land might appear under other names while remaining under the same effective control. Some of these benami arrangements concealed holdings above the legal ceiling, or maximum permitted holding. The cultivators and agricultural workers who knew a property’s everyday management could possess information that the official record obscured.[cite:konar-nielsen-land-power]

Kenneth Bo Nielsen’s account, drawing on administrator D. Bandyopadhyay, explains the method associated with Konar: combine legal provisions, public organisation and reliable testimony from villagers to expose concealed land and enforce reform. This gave practical importance to what poor people knew. It also sought to make administrative action strong enough to survive a change of government. By 1969, growing impatience with obstruction helped push the movement towards direct occupations, including actions beyond established legal procedures.[cite:konar-nielsen-land-power]

In his own account of the peasant movement, Konar judged progress partly through people’s greater confidence and capacity to organise. He described changes in peasants’ sense of dignity, alongside struggles over land and eviction. A later Bengali remembrance preserves the thought in a short saying attributed to him:[cite:konar-marxist-2015]

কৃষকরাই লড়াই করে জমি দখল করেছে।[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021]

The peasants themselves fought and took possession of the land.

This interpretation of his work keeps the cultivators in the story as participants with their own knowledge and courage. His political purpose was to make their collective action more effective. The land minister mattered because government could help make space for that action, while rural organisation could press government to act.[cite:konar-marxist-2015]

The conflicts that belong in the story

Naxalbari exposed a sharp conflict within the communist movement in 1967. Some local activists pursued an armed path that Konar and the CPI(M) leadership opposed. In a near-contemporary account, Gautan Appa described Konar’s unsuccessful efforts to reach agreement with the local cadres, while the party faced pressure from its United Front partners to maintain order. The dispute produced expulsions and a lasting political rupture.[cite:appa-1970]

The conflict had a terrible human cost. On 25 May 1967, police fired on demonstrators at Naxalbari, killing villagers, including women and children, while the United Front government held office. Later testimony recorded the anger felt towards communist leaders who had encouraged agrarian militancy but opposed its armed direction.[cite:konar-naxalbari-indian-express]

The disagreement placed the peasant organiser and the government minister in the same difficult position. Both sides claimed to act for the rural poor, but they disagreed over armed struggle, the role of the coalition government and the permissible means of change. Appa’s critical account is one perspective on this conflict. It helps explain why Konar’s ministerial career also attracted bitterness among people whose agrarian grievances he recognised.[cite:appa-1970]

There was another documented tension. Guha records that, as minister, Konar moved extensions of the land acquisition law on 28 March 1967 and 29 January 1970, having opposed its renewal in 1957. In office he retained a state power he had criticised in opposition. The record establishes that change of position; it does not by itself establish his personal motives.[cite:konar-guha-acquisition-law]

Arild Engelsen Ruud’s study of rural Bengal adds a wider qualification. Peasant mobilisation helped challenge the authority of landholders and gave villagers new protection, but it also strengthened the party’s power in local life. Negotiation, intimidation and compromises accompanied the gains. Empowerment was significant without being complete, and dependence on powerful patrons did not disappear simply because the balance shifted.[cite:ruud-1994]

The man remembered by others

Ashok Mitra recalled leaving his Delhi office to watch cricket and returning to discover that Konar had waited about an hour and gone away. Mitra felt deeply embarrassed: he believed a conversation with Konar could clarify agrarian conditions better than months of his own travel. The anecdote conveys an economist’s respect for knowledge acquired through organising.[cite:konar-ashok-mitra-2014]

Mitra also remembered the land reform specialist Wolf Ladejinsky asking him to bring Konar’s new Bengali books from Kolkata; Ladejinsky would arrange for translations. These recollections give a sense of Konar’s intellectual reach beyond the formal limits of his education. Mitra’s portrait also stresses warmth and a willingness to listen to criticism.[cite:konar-ashok-mitra-2014]

A lighter anecdote concerns the actor Shantigopal’s jatra about Lenin. An Anandabazar article recalls Konar telling the performer that one performance could accomplish what two hundred political speeches could not.The recollection offers an appealing glimpse of a celebrated speaker acknowledging the force of another art. Popular theatre could make political ideas accessible through feeling, character and a story that an audience could follow.[cite:konar-shantigopal-jatra]

An unfinished legacy

Konar died on 23 July 1974, shortly before his fifty-ninth birthday, three years before the Left Front took office.[cite:konar-sukanta-remembrance-2021][cite:benoy-land-reform-2004]

Operation Barga began in 1978. Its campaign to register sharecroppers and make their protections effective belongs to the later Left Front government. Land distribution and the registration of tenancy rights were related but different measures: a sharecropper could gain greater security while the land remained someone else’s property. Konar’s earlier work formed part of the political and organisational background of the later reforms; he did not personally carry out Operation Barga.[cite:benoy-land-reform-2004]

His legacy also concerns the relationships through which people approached power. Ruud’s research suggests that the Left’s expansion in the countryside cannot be explained by the quantity of land redistributed alone. The ability to challenge a landlord, obtain protection or enter a collective organisation also mattered. This helps explain Konar’s significance while giving due place to the villagers and local organisers whose actions made change possible.[cite:ruud-1994]

One of his own Bengali essays offers a fitting final perspective. Writing about Muzaffar Ahmad in January 1974, Konar admired a politics expressed in everyday conduct: simplicity, collective responsibility, care for comrades and freedom from self-promotion. He asked how fully people actually lived the ideals they professed. These were qualities he praised in another man, not claims he made about himself. They nevertheless show what he thought a political life ought to demand.[cite:konar-muzaffar-tribute]

That question can guide a public remembrance of Konar. His life invites attention to how conviction becomes conduct: in a household sharing the costs of political work, in the arrangements that deliver relief, in legislative proposals about a cultivator’s harvest, and in the difficult choices of government. His achievements become more intelligible when remembered alongside the people who worked with him and the conflicts he could not resolve.

Dutta names Konar among Ugra Kshatriya freedom-movement figures in Explorations in Colonial Bengal (2023), p. 314. His community connection is documented in that scholarship, as well as in Ashok Mitra’s remembrance.[cite:dutta-2023][cite:konar-ashok-mitra-2014]

Communist politics · Land reform · Rural development

Benoy Choudhury

1911–2000 · Revolutionary organiser and land reform minister[cite:benoy-choudhury-interview]

Benoy Choudhury was a revolutionary organiser, freedom fighter and communist leader who served as West Bengal’s land and land reforms minister from 1977 to 1996. He helped carry peasant demands into government through land reform and the protection of sharecroppers’ rights.[cite:benoy-choudhury-party][cite:benoy-amal-sarkar-remembrance]

A life in the struggle over land

For a sharecropper in rural Bengal, having a name entered in a government register could make a lasting difference. It strengthened the right to cultivate a field and offered protection against eviction. Benoy Choudhury helped bring these rights into the everyday lives of cultivators as West Bengal’s land and land reforms minister from 1977 to 1996.[cite:benoy-barga-economics][cite:benoy-amal-sarkar-remembrance]

Long before he became a minister, he had been a young revolutionary, a political prisoner and an organiser among workers and peasants. Born in 1911, he lived through the freedom struggle and the rise of the Left to government. His career connected the hopes of that earlier generation with the practical work of changing relations between landowners and cultivators.[cite:benoy-culture-profile][cite:benoy-choudhury-party][cite:benoy-choudhury-interview]

Family and early life

Choudhury was born at his maternal uncle’s home in Sutra, in the Monteswar area of Burdwan district. His ancestral village was Sihi. His father, Girindranath Choudhury, worked as a stamp vendor at Burdwan Court; his mother was Bilashini Devi. He attended Burdwan Municipal High School.[cite:benoy-culture-profile]

A later commemorative tribute describes a family with land and members engaged in business or service. It puts their joint holding at 165 bighas, an unconfirmed figure for the family as a whole.[cite:benoy-family-commemorative]

His wife was a school headmistress and died before him. Writing after Choudhury’s death, journalist Chanchal Sarkar relayed a friend’s memory of visiting him and finding him calculating the pension he might receive through his late wife. The small domestic scene gives a glimpse of his life beyond meetings and ministerial work.[cite:benoy-choudhury-tribune-2000]

From Jugantar to communism

In 1928, Choudhury entered Serampore College with his friend Saroj Mukherjee and joined the revolutionary organisation Jugantar. He later recalled how Vivekananda’s concern for poor and neglected people had influenced him. Through Vivekananda’s brother, Bhupendranath Datta, he encountered political teaching that linked the struggle for national freedom with the lives of workers and peasants.[cite:benoy-choudhury-vivekananda] He also recalled leading Burdwan’s Satyagrahi group in 1930, influenced by Vivekananda’s concern for ordinary people.[cite:benoy-choudhury-vivekananda]

Politics grew alongside a close friendship with Saroj. A Bengali remembrance recalls the two composing alternate lines of a poem, sharing literary interests as well as political commitments. They came into contact with communist organisers Abdul Halim and Muzaffar Ahmad. Muzaffar Ahmad later sent Choudhury to organise workers in Asansol.[cite:benoy-saroj-remembrance]

Choudhury was first arrested during the Civil Disobedience movement in 1930. Arrested again in the Birbhum conspiracy case in 1933, he received a five-year sentence. In prison he joined the circle of political prisoners known as the Communist Consolidation, studying communist ideas with fellow inmates. After his release in October 1938, he joined the Communist Party.[cite:benoy-choudhury-party]

Among workers and peasants

At Ballavpur, near Raniganj, Choudhury became involved in the paper-mill struggle of 1938–39. Workers demanded better wages and conditions, benefits and recognition of their union. He was among the organisers arrested during the strike. Women including Dasi Bauria, Bhagvantia and Yashovantia helped sustain the struggle through a volunteer organisation. Their efforts were part of the movement in which he worked.[cite:benoy-ballavpur-women]

He also took part in Burdwan’s campaign against taxation for canal irrigation. During the war, the British authorities excluded him from the district, and he continued working underground until the Communist Party became legal in 1942.[cite:benoy-choudhury-party]

Political education was another part of his work. Badruddin Umar remembered attending classes at the Burdwan Communist Party office, where Choudhury taught alongside Hare Krishna Konar and Syed Shahedullah. Umar went with relatives despite not belonging to the Student Federation himself. The party office was a place where young people could listen, learn and encounter new political ideas.[cite:benoy-umar-classes]

The election against the Maharaja

In 1952, Choudhury defeated Uday Chand Mahtab, the Maharaja of Burdwan, in the Burdwan Assembly constituency. The Communist candidate received 11,439 votes against the Congress candidate’s 9,477, winning by 1,962. A man who had spent years organising and enduring imprisonment entered the Assembly after defeating one of the district’s best-known public figures.[cite:benoy-election-1952]

When the Communist Party split in 1964, Choudhury joined the CPI(M). The Left Front’s arrival in government in 1977 brought him responsibility for land reform. He held that portfolio until 1996 and also served in the party’s Polit Bureau. His years among cultivators now informed the task of making government respond to their needs.[cite:benoy-choudhury-party][cite:benoy-amal-sarkar-remembrance][cite:benoy-polit-bureau-register]

Earlier, as leader of the Krishak Samiti in Burdwan after the first United Front government took office in 1967, he toured village meetings across Raina denouncing the looting that had spread there: “Looting is not our ideology” (Ruud, pp. 109–110).[cite:ruud-2003]

Land reform in village life

Operation Barga began in 1978. Its purpose was to record bargadars, or sharecroppers, and make their legal protections effective. Officials held camps in villages, explained the law and checked who was cultivating particular fields. Registration came closer to people who might hesitate to visit an office or fear displeasing a landowner. Cultivators, organisers and officials all helped put the programme into practice.[cite:benoy-barga-economics]

Registration protected the right to sharecrop; ownership came through the separate process of land redistribution. Choudhury’s priorities included both recording bargadars and recovering concealed surplus holdings. The crop-sharing provisions entitled a cultivator who supplied the required inputs to three-quarters of the produce. Where the owner supplied them, the prescribed division was half and half.[cite:benoy-kurukshetra-1995]

By 1995, the statewide total of registered bargadars had reached about 14.7 lakh, including earlier registrations. Land reform worked alongside improvements in irrigation, agricultural inputs and local institutions. It also left difficult questions unresolved, especially for agricultural labourers and women seeking rights to land. Choudhury’s work formed part of this wider effort to change rural life.[cite:benoy-land-reform-2004]

The minister people remembered

Journalist Amal Sarkar remembered a minister who arrived at functions on time and, if the organisers were not ready, quietly waited in an audience chair. Sarkar also recalled Choudhury refusing a proposed government-funded inverter for his flat. These small encounters stayed in the memories of people who knew him.[cite:benoy-amal-sarkar-remembrance]

His long service did not prevent him from criticising his own government. Towards the end of his ministerial career, he publicly challenged the growing influence of contractors. The contemporary Left journal Liberation reported his criticism of the relationship between contractors and panchayat leadership. The interests served by government remained a concern even after decades in public life.[cite:benoy-liberation-1997]

His later years

In June 1992, Andrew Whitehead interviewed Choudhury in Calcutta for the radio series What’s Left of Communism? The recording preserves his voice recalling Jugantar, prison, peasant politics and land reform. His English memoir, My Life & Experiences, appeared in 1999.[cite:benoy-choudhury-interview][cite:benoy-my-life-experiences]

Choudhury died on 6 May 2000. His body was donated to Nilratan Sarkar Medical College and Hospital for medical teaching.[cite:benoy-choudhury-party][cite:benoy-body-donation-2024]

The University of Burdwan renamed its Rural Technology Centre in his memory in 2000; the centre supports practical training and rural livelihoods.[cite:benoy-choudhury-university]

Ajit Kumar Panja

1936–2008 · Former Union minister

Ajit Kumar Panja brought legal training and an interest in the arts to public life. Born in Calcutta, he studied at Scottish Church College and Lincoln’s Inn, London. Before entering the Lok Sabha in 1984, he served in West Bengal’s Assembly and government, including responsibility for health and family planning in 1972–1977.[cite:ajit-bio]

He represented Calcutta North-East in six successive Lok Sabhas, from the Eighth to the Thirteenth (1984–2004). His Union portfolios included information and broadcasting, finance, coal and external affairs. Parliament’s obituary also remembered his Bengali and English writing and his work on the stage.[cite:ajit-obituary]

His books included Sansad Bichitra and a handbook for health and mine workers. In June 1992 he led a delegation to Britain, France and Germany to study parliamentary broadcasting.[cite:ajit-bio]

Freedom movement · Law · Public service

Jadabendra Nath Panja

1886–21 September 1961 · Freedom fighter and state minister

The poet Shankha Ghosh remembered a visit to a modest room near College Street in Calcutta in 1950. The room contained two floor mats, modest bedding and tin suitcases. While Ghosh spoke to the revolutionary Kiran Chandra Mukherjee, another man was resting nearby. Reminded that it was time to leave, the man got up, put on a worn panjabi and left.[cite:panja-shankha-ghosh-recollection]

Ghosh was astonished when he learnt who the departing lodger was: Jadabendra Nath Panja, a minister in the West Bengal government, on his way to Writers’ Buildings. Kiran’s response was:

“মন্ত্রী তো কী হলো, সেটা কোনো কথা নয়, ও হলো যাদবেন্দ্র পাঁজা।”

“Minister? That changes nothing; he is Jadabendra Panja.”[cite:panja-shankha-ghosh-recollection]

The scene captures something that stayed with those who remembered him. Public office had brought him responsibility; his daily life remained remarkably modest.[cite:panja-shankha-ghosh-recollection]

Panja’s concern for other people was equally visible in his public work. He took up the cases of cultivators losing their land, questioned the burdens placed on those who needed irrigation, and pressed government to restore fields damaged by floods. Among the Aguris, he is remembered for representing the community in a dispute over its official classification. His humanism took shape in these particular responsibilities, through the people and problems to which he gave his attention.[cite:panja-family-oral-history][cite:panja-dinabandhu-community-account][cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2][cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-3][cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-2]

The life he chose

Born in 1886, Panja belonged to Satinandi in Burdwan district. The Panja family of Sathinandi, Burdwan was one of the prominent 19th century Aguri families which took a Dar-patni tenure. Writing about the 1930s, historian Achintya Kumar Dutta records that Panja’s family possessed 1,500 bighas of land in Burdwan. His father, Nrisinghamurari Panja, was a teacher at Burdwan Raj School. Panja passed the F.A. examination at Burdwan Raj College, studied at Ripon and Scottish Church colleges in Calcutta, and obtained a law degree. He began practising in Burdwan. The local historian Sudhir Chandra Da also records his contact with Ripon College’s principal, Ramendrasundar Trivedi.[cite:panja-bardhaman-parikrama][cite:panja-jangipur-obituary-1961][cite:panja-dutta-economy-ecology]

He carried intellectual interests into political life. Atulya Ghosh remembered his knowledge of philosophy and Rabindranath Tagore. In 1921, answering the call of Non-Cooperation, Panja gave up legal practice. He never returned to it. He had exchanged legal practice for the uncertain work of the freedom movement.[cite:panja-jangipur-obituary-1961][cite:panja-atulya-ghosh-memoir][cite:panja-district-history-2000]

That work often meant explaining national events in villages and encouraging people to participate. During the Salt Satyagraha of 1930, Bengali local histories place Panja among the organisers who carried the movement into Burdwan’s countryside. Ekkari Chattopadhyay records his leadership of twenty-five volunteers going to Mahishbathan. Sudhir Chandra Da describes a wider campaign of village meetings, spinning and weaving. The movement needed people who could make its purpose understood in everyday life.[cite:panja-bardhaman-parikrama][cite:panja-district-history-2000]

The commitment brought punishment into his own life. In January 1932, Panja was convicted of promoting a meeting of the banned Burdwan Congress Committee. He received two and a half years’ rigorous imprisonment and a fine of Rs 500, with another six months in prison if the fine was not paid. Some of his movable property was seized and sold.[cite:panja-high-court-1936]

Panja later maintained that he had served the full additional term even though part of the fine had been recovered. A further attempt to collect the balance led to a court challenge. In January 1936, the Calcutta High Court set aside that later recovery warrant. The legal record gives a stark sense of what political commitment could cost: imprisonment, the loss of possessions and a dispute that continued after release. He was imprisoned again in 1942.[cite:panja-high-court-1936][cite:jadabendra-archive]

Alongside such sacrifices was the quieter work of caring for fellow workers. Atulya Ghosh recalled the district Congress office as a place where they found food and lodging, with Panja looking after them like a guardian. Political organisation depended on these ordinary acts of support as well as on speeches and campaigns.[cite:panja-atulya-ghosh-memoir]

Taking the cultivator’s side

In the Bengal Legislative Assembly, Panja repeatedly brought the experience of rural people into discussions of government policy. A field taken for a public project, a charge for canal water or land left under flood deposits could determine a family’s ability to make a living. He asked ministers to account for such consequences.[cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2][cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-3][cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-2]

In August 1946, he spoke for cultivators around Panagarh whose land had been taken during the war. They had expected it to be returned when the war ended. Panja complained that compensation had been inadequate and delayed. He also challenged further acquisition around the Hirapur ironworks while previously acquired land remained unused. His argument required government to reckon with the people who were giving up their land.[cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2]

The same concern appeared in his questions about the Damodar Canal. In September 1946, he challenged the rise in the irrigation charge from two rupees nine annas to four rupees eight annas an acre. Why, he asked, was a higher charge necessary when the canal accounts showed an annual surplus? The minister pointed to rising maintenance costs and agricultural prices. Panja had placed the cultivator’s burden before the Assembly and required an explanation.[cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-3]

Flood damage raised another difficulty. A field buried under sand could remain a holding on paper while ceasing to provide a crop. In March 1947, Panja pressed for the reclamation of damaged land, particularly in Burdwan. The minister replied that removing the sand would be prohibitively expensive and that recovery through the deposit of fresh silt was being studied. The farmer’s need to get the land back into cultivation was the problem Panja had brought to government.[cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-2]

He also pressed for the unfinished bridge over the Damodar on the Burdwan–Arambagh road. During the monsoon, he told the Assembly, communication with Raina, Khandaghosh and Arambagh was cut off. He contrasted the elaborate foundation ceremony with the lack of progress. He urged completion before people could say:

“it was begun in flash and ended in smoke.”[cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2]

The phrase preserves his humour and impatience with ceremonial promises.

His August budget cut motions were defeated. His later motion on flood reclamation met the same outcome. Even so, his interventions kept the losses and difficulties of rural communities before the provincial government. He insisted that officials explain decisions which could otherwise remain remote from those most affected by them.[cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2][cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-2]

That concern extended to the use of public money. In the budget debate of February 1947, Panja asked what increased expenditure had achieved for health, education and living standards. He pressed for technical education, restoration of derelict tanks and irrigation works in Burdwan Division, and supported the Damodar Valley Project. He also criticised unequal distribution under Civil Supplies, the department responsible for food and other essential supplies, and wanted separate accounts and a separate budget so that its spending could be examined. The finance minister defended its subsidised food provision but did not accept the separate-budget demand.[cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-1]

Standing beside the Aguris

Panja also holds a place in the history of the Aguri, or Ugra Kshatriya, community to which he belonged. Here, his service is remembered through a dispute about how the community should be classified by government.[cite:panja-dutta-economy-ecology]

According to the family account passed down by the contributor’s father, efforts were made in the 1930s to place the Aguris among the backward classes or Scheduled Castes. Panja took up the community’s case, opposed the proposed classification and secured the correction it sought. In this oral history, his intervention stands as an example of a leader accepting responsibility for concerns that mattered deeply to the community.[cite:panja-family-oral-history]

A related account appears in Dinabandhu Panja’s Bengali article “উগ্রক্ষত্রিয় জাতির সামাজিক সংস্কার”, published by History and Culture of Ugra-Khastriya. It also credits Panja with leadership in the effort against Scheduled Caste inclusion. These accounts preserve his place as a community representative who was prepared to take its case to the authorities.[cite:panja-dinabandhu-community-account]

Disputes over official descriptions had a longer history. The 1931 census records objections to the name “Aguri” and an agreement to use “Ugra Kshattriya (Aguri)”. For the community, such representations were a way of having its understanding of its identity heard by government.[cite:panja-census-1931-name]

The later census records provide supporting context. An administrative inventory for the 1951 census identifies a file called “Caste Aguri”, numbered 5C-10.Nearby entries concern scheduled castes, scheduled tribes and backward classes. The published tables list “Ugra Kshatria” among the castes declared non-backward by the state government for census purposes. Panja’s part in securing a correction is preserved in the family and community accounts; these official records document the community’s administrative history.[cite:panja-census-1951-inventory][cite:panja-census-1951-tables]

His work for the Aguris belongs within a life spent representing people before institutions that held power over them. In the community’s remembrance, he was someone who took its concerns seriously and helped obtain the response it sought.[cite:panja-family-oral-history][cite:panja-dinabandhu-community-account]

The responsibilities of office

Panja entered government during Bengal’s difficult passage to independence. On 20 June 1947, he voted with the Assembly section representing the predominantly non-Muslim areas for the province’s partition and for West Bengal to join India’s existing Constituent Assembly. The vote placed him among those who supported the creation of West Bengal within India.[cite:panja-partition-votes-1947]

The weeks before Independence Day also brought a test of solidarity. Atulya Ghosh recalled that, when the young Scheduled Caste minister Radhanath Das faced exclusion from the ministry, Panja and several colleagues immediately wrote resignation letters. A temporary compromise followed. Panja had joined a protest in which his own position in office was at stake.[cite:panja-atulya-ghosh-memoir]

In May 1948, Panja held Agriculture, Veterinary, Forests and Fisheries in B. C. Roy’s cabinet. He also served as minister for Cottage and Small-scale Industries. His responsibilities thus included fields of government closely connected with rural livelihoods. At the first general election, in 1951–52, he was returned from Galsi, then a constituency electing two members, alongside Mahitosh Saha.[cite:jadabendra-archive][cite:panja-cabinet-report-1948][cite:panja-election-galsi-1951]

His political responsibilities continued into old age. He served as president of the West Bengal Pradesh Congress Committee from 1958 until his death.[cite:jadabendra-archive]

Through these changes in office, he retained the simplicity for which Burdwan remembered him. Indrajit Chowdhury’s Bengali account records that Panja stopped wearing shoes when he joined the Congress in 1920 and continued to go barefoot even as a minister. The local name “Burdwan’s Gandhi” drew meaning from such habits and from his long commitment to the freedom movement.[cite:panja-barefoot-remembrance-2019]

Remembering the man

Panja died at his home in Satinandi on 21 September 1961. His wife had died about a year earlier. He left two daughters and grandchildren. The contemporary obituary describes Congress leaders arranging for his body to be brought to the district office and for his daughters to come from Calcutta before the funeral procession. His family and the organisation to which he had given so much came together in that farewell.[cite:panja-jangipur-obituary-1961]

The people in the story of his public life give his legacy its human scale: the Congress worker needing food and a place to sleep, the cultivator waiting for compensation, the family dependent on flood-damaged land, the colleague facing exclusion and the community seeking a hearing. Panja met these concerns through personal care, protest and representation. That is the life of service behind the modest figure Shankha Ghosh watched leave for work.[cite:panja-shankha-ghosh-recollection][cite:panja-family-oral-history][cite:panja-dinabandhu-community-account][cite:panja-assembly-1946-part-2][cite:panja-assembly-1947-part-2][cite:panja-jangipur-obituary-1961][cite:panja-atulya-ghosh-memoir][cite:panja-high-court-1936]

Dermatology · Medical research · Publishing

Dr Ganapati Panja

1891–1959

When a skin outpatient clinic opened at the Calcutta School of Tropical Medicine in 1922, Ganapati Panja was at the beginning of a career that would help shape the study of skin disease in India. He had joined the school as an assistant professor in 1921. Working alongside Hugh W. Acton, he helped establish its dermatology department in 1923, bringing the care of patients into close contact with teaching and laboratory investigation. [1][3][4]

The road from Majigram

The road to that appointment began in Majigram, in the former Burdwan district. Baridbaran Ghosh’s Bengali biography describes a childhood marked by poverty and orphanhood at about seven. It credits Maharaja Manindra Chandra Nandy of Cossimbazar with supporting Ganapati and his younger brother, Dhanapati, and enabling Ganapati’s education at Hindu School, Presidency College and Calcutta Medical College. He qualified in 1919. [1]

His origins also connect his story with the history of the Aguri community. Historian Achintya Kumar Dutta includes Panja among its intellectual figures. [2]

From the patient to the microscope

One of his early research problems concerned seborrhoeic dermatitis: inflamed, flaky skin, often around the scalp and other oil-rich areas. Dandruff is a familiar, milder form of scalp scaling. Researchers were investigating the association of these conditions with Malassezia, microscopic yeasts that can live on human skin. [5][9]

In 1927, Panja achieved what a major historical review by H. Ruth Ashbee and E. Glyn V. Evans describes as the generally accepted first successful laboratory isolation of the organism, while acknowledging earlier claims. Isolation meant obtaining it from a sample so that it could be grown and examined separately. Panja had not discovered the yeast itself, which was already known; he had helped make it accessible to experimental study. His work belonged to a wider, sometimes disputed effort to distinguish and classify related fungal forms. [5]

A Rockefeller scholarship took him to London for bacteriology research in 1932, extending his study of microbes. [1] The distinction between finding a microbe and establishing it as the cause of disease became central to his 1936 paper, “Infantile Eczema”. Eczema means inflamed, often itchy skin. Drawing on experience of about 250 cases, he examined 30 in greater detail. Of 23 children whose skin or scalp scrapings were tested, none showed yeast under direct microscopic examination, and only three samples produced cultures. These observations led him to question the idea that infantile eczema generally began as a yeast-associated scalp disorder. [7]

His interests extended beyond those yeasts. With C. L. Pasricha, he studied diphtheritic skin ulcers known as “Garigha” in the Chittagong Hill Tracts in 1940. Diphtheria is usually associated with the throat, but its bacterium can also occur in skin wounds. A later United States Army medical history discussed their findings, while noting that an experimental conclusion still needed confirmation. [10]

Meanwhile, growing Malassezia in sufficient quantity remained a practical obstacle: earlier cultures produced only tiny, sparse colonies. Following Rhoda Benham’s 1939 demonstration that the yeast needed fatty substances, Panja described an oil-enriched medium on which it grew more abundantly. A medium supplies the nutrients an organism needs in the laboratory. More reliable growth meant more material for comparison and experiment. He presented this work at the Indian Science Congress in Benares in 1941 and published it in 1946. [5][6]

By 1946, he held the title of Professor of Bacteriology and Pathology and the London D.Bact. qualification. Those disciplines brought complementary tools to his work: bacteriology investigates bacteria, while pathology examines how illness changes the body’s tissues. [6]

A rare infection and a new journal

In March 1954, Panja and S. Sen published “A unique case of histoplasmosis” in the Journal of the Indian Medical Association. Later research identifies their Calcutta report as India’s first reported case of disseminated histoplasmosis. [8][11]

Histoplasmosis is caused by the fungus Histoplasma; “disseminated” means that the infection has spread through the body rather than remaining in one place. Their report gave other doctors a documented case against which to compare their observations. [8][11]

The following year, Panja took on a larger role in publishing the work of his profession. In June 1955, he became editor-in-chief for dermatology at the Indian Journal of Dermatology and Venereology, with R. V. Rajam responsible for venereology. In October he launched the separate Indian Journal of Dermatology. The new journal provided a forum for clinical observations and research. He remained its editor for the rest of his life. [4]

Beyond his professional work

His commitment to education also reached back to his home village. He established Bisweswari High School and Binodbehari Panja Lower Basic School in memory of his parents. Ghosh’s biography records his death on 7 September 1959. [1]

The family’s wider public life

Medicine remained important in the family, but it was not the only route to public distinction. Dhanapati also became a dermatologist. His sons, Ranjit and Ajit, were Ganapati’s nephews; Ganapati’s own son was Salil. [1][13][14][16]

Dr Ranjit Kumar Panja (1932–2006) combined clinical teaching with elected office. Trained at Calcutta Medical College and the Royal College of Physicians, Edinburgh, he served as Professor of Dermatology at Calcutta Medical College from 1974 to 1990 and became president of the Indian Association of Dermatologists, Venereologists and Leprologists. Elected to the Lok Sabha from Barasat in 1998, he served on the Human Resource Development Committee and its subcommittee on medical education. He was re-elected in 1999. [14][18]

His brother Ajit Kumar Panja (1936–2008) pursued law and politics. Educated at Scottish Church College and Lincoln’s Inn, the barrister held ministerial office in West Bengal before representing Calcutta North-East in six successive Lok Sabhas from 1984 to 2004. His Union responsibilities included Information and Broadcasting, Finance, Coal and External Affairs. Parliament’s obituary also recalled his books and stage acting, reflecting a public presence that extended into Bengali cultural life. [13][15]

Their cousin, Professor Salil Kumar Panja (1934–2023), headed dermatology departments at IPGMER and SSKM Hospital and at Calcutta National Medical College, helping develop specialist teaching and postgraduate training. He also edited the Indian Journal of Dermatology and served as a national president of the dermatologists’ association. [16]

In a later generation, Dr Shashi Panja, Ajit’s daughter-in-law and a physician by training, entered public administration. Her ministerial responsibilities in West Bengal included women and child development and social welfare in 2021, and industry in 2023. [17]

A lasting place in the profession

After Ganapati’s death, the journal continued to develop under his successors, gaining inclusion in Index Medicus in 1962. [4] The Professor Ganapati Panja Memorial Award, maintained by the Indian Association of Dermatologists, Venereologists and Leprologists, gives his name an enduring place in the profession. [12]

Mathematics · Research · Education

Dr Manohar Ray

From Nasigram to the mathematics of flow.

How does an oval cylinder move through liquid whose neighbouring layers travel at different speeds? Manohar Ray studied this interaction between a solid body and the fluid around it, developing a way to calculate the pressure and work out the body’s motion.[cite:manohar-paper-1937]

An oval in a moving fluid

Picture layers of liquid sliding in the same direction, with speed changing steadily from one layer to the next. This is uniform shear, one of the flows Ray examined. His oval is the cross-section of an idealised cylinder extending without end; the model leaves out the complications at a real object’s ends.[cite:manohar-paper-1937]

Ray treated the liquid as ideal, without viscosity. His paper connected a pressure calculation with equations for the cylinder’s movement. The technical title was Motion of an Infinite Elliptic Cylinder in Fluids with Constant Vorticity. “Vorticity” describes local rotation within a fluid; it does not require every fluid particle to follow a circular path.[cite:manohar-paper-1937]

Criticism, revision and a second reading

The Royal Society preserves Geoffrey Ingram Taylor’s referee reports, including his objections to the submitted version. In the first report, received on 24 April 1936, he did not recommend publication and required changes. In the accompanying criticism, Taylor argued that Ray had missed the general reach of an earlier solution. He also objected that a formula for the turning effect left out angular acceleration—how quickly the rate of rotation changes. These are Taylor’s criticisms of that earlier version, not a judgment here on the final published paper.[cite:manohar-first-review]

Ray revised the work. Taylor’s second report recommended publication in Proceedings, in full, with no material modifications. His handwritten assessment was:

“This paper is much better than the original version.”

G. I. Taylor, second referee report, received 31 August 1936.[cite:manohar-royal-society]

The reports show more than a decision to print a paper. Taylor tested its relationship to earlier research and the consequences of its mathematics; the revised submission then received a different judgment. That is the editorial reading of this documented sequence. The reports do not, by themselves, establish every alteration Ray made between versions.

The paper appeared in Proceedings of the Royal Society A on 3 February 1937, in volume 158, pages 522–535. The April and August dates are receipt dates stamped on the referee reports, not publication or acceptance dates for Ray’s paper.[cite:manohar-first-review][cite:manohar-royal-society][cite:manohar-paper-1937]

Nasigram and the path into scholarship

Ray was a mathematician, teacher and textbook author from Nasigram whose academic career connected rural Bardhaman with Lahore and Agra. His work survives in scientific archives, college records and the mathematics books that carried his name into classrooms.[cite:dutta-2023][cite:manohar-fcc-folio][cite:manohar-college][cite:manohar-hydrostatics]

Historian Achintya Kumar Dutta identifies the mathematician Manohar Roy of Nasigram among the Ugra Kshatriya intellectual figures in his study of social mobility in southwest Bengal. This village connection places Ray within a history in which education opened professional possibilities alongside agriculture, landholding and commerce.[cite:dutta-2023]

The beginnings of that journey remain less clear than his later career. By the 1930s, however, his research had reached the Royal Society in London.[cite:manohar-first-review]

Professor of Mathematics in Lahore

Forman Christian College’s archive provides a dated glimpse of Ray as a teacher. Its catalogue for the October–November 1940 issue of The Folio identifies him as Professor of Mathematics and records an illustration of him with Professor Mathai.[cite:manohar-fcc-folio]

Lahore was then part of undivided India. Ray’s presence there helps us see his career across the geography of the time, extending beyond Bengal into a wider academic world. The magazine establishes his position in 1940; it does not establish when he joined or left the college.

A decade at the head of Agra College

Agra College’s official list records Ray as principal from 1960 to 1970, with the qualifications M.Sc., Ph.D., D.Sc., F.N.I. and F.N.A.Sc. It is the clearest institutional record of his later leadership.[cite:manohar-college] The publisher Ratan Prakashan Mandir also describes him as a former Registrar of Agra University and associates his D.Sc. with the University of Calcutta. The available college record identifies his principalship at Agra College; the publisher’s additional claims still need confirmation from university records.[cite:manohar-ratan]

A continuing life through textbooks

Ray’s writing also addressed students learning mathematics and mechanics. A library catalogue records the fifth revised and enlarged edition of his A Text Book of Hydrostatics for B.A. and B.Sc. Students, published by Premier Publishing Company in Delhi in 1966, with 224 pages. The edition history indicates repeated publication.[cite:manohar-hydrostatics]

In the publisher catalogue checked for this profile, Ratan Prakashan Mandir still lists Ganit books for Classes 9 and 10 under his name. These later titles show the continuing presence of his name in school mathematics publishing.[cite:manohar-ratan]

The research paper and the textbooks preserve different sides of an academic career: the specialist developing a mathematical argument and the teacher addressing a wider readership. In the community history outlined by Dutta, Ray offers an example of the opportunities opened by higher education and professional scholarship.[cite:dutta-2023]

Bengali & Hindi cinema

Shakti Samanta

13 January 1926 – 9 April 2009 · Director and producer · Founder of Shakti Films[cite:shakti-cinemaazi-profile]

Shakti Samanta helped shape the appeal of popular Indian cinema through films such as Kashmir Ki Kali, Aradhana, Kati Patang and Amar Prem. His partnerships with Shammi Kapoor, Rajesh Khanna and Sharmila Tagore brought together memorable performances and music. Across his work, adventurous romances sit alongside stories of bereavement, loneliness and the search for affection.[cite:shakti-ashim-rediff-2012]

Bokra and a childhood shaped by loss

Samanta was born on 13 January 1926 in Bokra (বোকরা) in an Aguri family, in the Raina area of the old Burdwan district, now in Purba Bardhaman, West Bengal. His ancestral connection is remembered locally: a Bengali report published by The Wall in August 2025 drew attention to visitors coming to Bokra to see the family house.[cite:shakti-tuli-profile][cite:shakti-bokra-house-2025]

According to his son, filmmaker Ashim Samanta, the family was financially comfortable, and Shakti’s father and uncles were engineers. His childhood was nevertheless marked by an early tragedy: his father died in an accident when Shakti was only two. An uncle subsequently brought him up. His early life combined the advantages of an educated professional household with the loss of a parent while he was still a small child.[cite:shakti-ashim-amanush-2026]

He spent formative years in Dehradun, where his education gave him a strong command of Hindi. Theatre and music were part of his early life; Ashim remembered that his father acted in college productions and played the flute. These interests would eventually meet in a profession that required him to understand actors, dialogue and music together.[cite:shakti-centenary-scroll-2026]

There was also a path he did not take. In an interview with East India Story, Ashim recalled that Shakti had wanted to join the Air Force and had passed its selection, but his mother objected. His mother, already widowed, refused her permission.[cite:shakti-ashim-east-india-2022]

The teacher who wanted to act

Shakti’s ambition was to become an actor. To support himself while pursuing work in Bombay, he took a teaching job at Dapoli in Maharashtra’s Konkan region. Film writer Shoma A Chatterji describes him making regular visits to the city on his school’s Friday holiday, watching shoots and trying to find an opening. The job provided a livelihood while those studio visits allowed him to learn what the film world actually involved.[cite:shakti-upperstall-2016]

Ashok Kumar became an important guide. The established actor advised the newcomer to consider assisting directors, and Samanta began an apprenticeship that included Satish Nigam, Phani Majumdar and Gyan Mukherjee. His early work included Sunhere Din, released in 1949, followed by experience on productions such as Tamasha and Baadbaan. Bombay’s Bengali filmmakers offered a setting in which his language skills could become a practical advantage.[cite:shakti-tuli-profile]

He could translate scenes written in Bengali into fluent Hindi, helping directors communicate their ideas to a wider production team. Ashim also recalled an audacious beginning: when asked whether he knew editing, Shakti said yes, then hurried to learn by watching an editor friend at work. Writing, editing and assisting gradually drew him towards directing.[cite:shakti-centenary-scroll-2026]

His brief teaching career was remembered long afterwards. Ashim recalled approaching the Shipping Corporation of India for permission to film aboard a ship and meeting an executive who had been his father’s pupil at Dapoli. The man spoke warmly of the young teacher’s lasting influence.[cite:shakti-ashim-amanush-2026]

A neighbour and the first directing opportunity

His first released feature as director was Bahu in 1955. The circumstances were preserved in another family’s memory. Sundeep Pahwa, whose father Basant Pahwa knew Samanta, told Cinemaazi that the two men were neighbours in Khar, Bombay. Basant’s brother Bikram was producing Bahu. The intended director, Vrajendra Gaur, could not take the assignment because of an earlier commitment to Kasturi, and Basant recommended Samanta.[cite:shakti-bahu-memory]

The episode shows the importance of ordinary acquaintance in a career often described only through its later triumphs. Samanta had learned enough to be ready, but somebody still had to trust him with a whole film. Bahu, starring Karan Dewan, Usha Kiran, Shashikala and Pran, gave him that opportunity. Inspector followed in 1956 and strengthened his position as a director.[cite:shakti-bahu-memory]

In 1957 he established Shakti Films. Its first production, Howrah Bridge, appeared in 1958 with Ashok Kumar and Madhubala. The combination of crime, suspense and music became an early signature. The company also gave him a continuing base from which to develop and produce films, with greater control over his own projects.[cite:shakti-studio]

Music, glamour and a feel for audiences

The 1960s brought a succession of very different entertainments. China Town used Shammi Kapoor in a crime story; Kashmir Ki Kali, released in 1964, introduced Sharmila Tagore to Hindi cinema; An Evening in Paris followed in 1967 with romance and foreign locations. Samanta’s partnership with Shammi Kapoor helped create a screen world of movement, mischief and exuberant courtship. His work with Sharmila would develop into a much longer association, reaching well beyond these early glamorous roles.[cite:shakti-upperstall-2016]

Music was central to the way he thought about films. In conversations with Peeyush Sharma, later published in Silhouette, Samanta explained that radio songs could make audiences want to see a film before they knew much about its story. He described choosing tunes through discussion with composers and lyricists. His experience with Howrah Bridge, with music by O P Nayyar, reinforced his belief that popular songs could draw audiences into cinemas. His commercial instinct rested on close attention to what people enjoyed hearing.[cite:shakti-silhouette-2016]

That attention extended to the camera. In Aradhana, the song Roop Tera Mastana unfolds in a continuous shot. Ashim recalled that his father felt a cut would interrupt the growing attraction between the characters. The technique served the moment: the viewer stays with the pair as the tension develops.[cite:shakti-centenary-scroll-2026]

Sharmila Tagore remembered another, more practical solution. During Mere Sapnon Ki Rani, she and Rajesh Khanna were filmed separately because her dates clashed with Satyajit Ray’s Aranyer Din Ratri. The finished sequence brings their performances together. She later said that Samanta understood why she could not turn down Ray. The two film worlds were often connected through these everyday negotiations.[cite:shakti-sharmila-aradhana-2025]

Aradhana and the turn towards emotional drama

Aradhana, released in 1969, became a decisive film in the rise of Rajesh Khanna. It also gave Sharmila Tagore a demanding central role as Vandana, whose love, bereavement and struggle to remain close to her son carry the story. Khanna played both the father and the grown son. Sachin Dev Burman’s music, Anand Bakshi’s lyrics and Kishore Kumar’s singing were integral to its appeal. Behind the romance lay a story about the harsh consequences of a relationship denied recognition by family and society.[cite:shakti-aradhana-film]

Sharmila later remembered a tense audience screening. When she first appeared in her older make-up, people giggled, and Samanta feared that they had rejected the performance. A subsequent scene changed the mood; by the end, viewers were moved to tears. It is a telling glimpse of an experienced filmmaker still anxiously watching to see whether an audience would believe his story.[cite:shakti-sharmila-aradhana-2025]

Kati Patang (1971) followed, with Asha Parekh and Rajesh Khanna. Based on a story by Gulshan Nanda, it brought together another emotionally troubled heroine and an R D Burman score. Alongside Aradhana, it forms part of the period in which Samanta’s name became closely associated with Khanna’s romantic screen presence.[cite:shakti-kati-patang-map]

Bengali stories and the people at their heart

Amar Prem, released in 1972, made that connection especially clear. Its story came from Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay and had already reached Bengali cinema as Nishi Padma. Samanta’s version centred on Pushpa, played by Sharmila Tagore, a woman driven from her home and into a Calcutta brothel. Rajesh Khanna’s lonely Anand Babu finds companionship with her, while her bond with a child opens another kind of emotional belonging. R D Burman composed the music.[cite:shakti-amar-prem-film][cite:shakti-silhouette-2016]

The film invites viewers to look beyond Pushpa’s social label and recognise her capacity for affection. That is a useful way to understand Samanta’s achievement as a popular storyteller. His plots could be highly dramatic, but they made a broad audience care about people who had lost family protection or social respect. The question of who deserves tenderness matters as much as the question of who will marry whom.[cite:shakti-amar-prem-film]

This interest had a literary foundation. Ashim remembered his father’s enthusiasm for Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay and his advice to read those stories for their understanding of human emotion. The connection helps explain why Bengali literature remained a resource for him even after he had become firmly established in Bombay.[cite:shakti-ashim-east-india-2022]

Uttam Kumar and the making of Amanush

With Amanush, Samanta brought his Bengali connections into the centre of a major production. Uttam Kumar and Sharmila Tagore led the cast, with Shaktipada Rajguru collaborating on the screenplay and Shyamal Mitra composing the music. The Bengali version opened on 18 October 1974; the Hindi version followed in 1975. Making versions for both audiences gave the production a reach that a single-language release could not have offered.[cite:shakti-amanush-bengali][cite:shakti-tuli-profile]

Ashim’s Bengali memoir in Robbar preserves a vivid story of the location search. His father travelled with Rajguru to see the Sundarbans setting described in the novel Naya Basat. At first the landscape disappointed him. An uncomfortable, mosquito-filled night aboard their boat changed that: awake at dawn, he watched the sky and river take on new colours and decided to film there.[cite:shakti-amanush-robbar-2026]

The decision required substantial preparation. Ashim describes accommodation and working facilities being built for a large unit, including kitchens, sanitation and generators. His recollection of the release is equally striking. Nervous about how Bengali viewers would respond, Samanta watched from the projection room. When the audience discovered him, they carried him on their shoulders.[cite:shakti-amanush-robbar-2026]

Ashim also recalled that the houses built for the Amanush film unit were later donated to local villagers. Tourists attracted by the film stayed in them, bringing income to the settlement. When the filmmakers returned for Ananda Ashram, the houses were repaired. His account describes assistance to the Sundarbans village where the unit had worked.[cite:shakti-ashim-amanush-2026]

Ananda Ashram, released in 1977, reunited Uttam Kumar and Sharmila Tagore within a cast that also included Ashok Kumar, Moushumi Chatterjee and Utpal Dutt. Anusandhan followed in 1981, with Amitabh Bachchan, Rakhee and Amjad Khan; its Hindi counterpart was Barsaat Ki Ek Raat. These films made the exchange of performers, music and production experience between Bombay and Bengal a continuing part of his work.[cite:shakti-ananda-ashram-film][cite:shakti-anusandhan-film]

Anyay Abichar, released in 1985, extended that work into an India–Bangladesh co-production, pairing Mithun Chakraborty with Bangladeshi actress Rozina. The film again drew on a story by Shaktipada Rajguru. Samanta’s career therefore belongs to several overlapping film histories: Hindi popular cinema, Bengali commercial cinema and collaborations across the Bengal border.[cite:shakti-anyay-abichar-film][cite:shakti-anyay-coproduction]

Family and a home filled with cinema

Shakti Samanta’s wife was Nilima Samanta. They had two sons, Ashim and Samir. His brother Girija Samanta was also closely involved in his production work. Ashim followed his father into directing and producing, continuing the family’s involvement in films and later in television and dubbing.[cite:shakti-cinemaazi-profile][cite:shakti-silhouette-2016][cite:shakti-studio]

Ashim remembered music sessions at home, where his father became absorbed in discussions of tunes and songs. Composers R D Burman and O P Nayyar and lyricist Anand Bakshi became close friends. Work and family life overlapped, and Ashim later described his father as a friend as well as a parent. The films were part of the family’s shared life as well as its occupation.[cite:shakti-ashim-rediff-2012]

The next generation continued that connection. Ashim’s son Aditya entered acting with Yeh Jo Mohabbat Hai in 2012. Aditya told the Press Trust of India that he had originally preferred production and direction, but his grandfather saw him in a play and encouraged his father to cast him as a leading actor. Shakti died before the project reached audiences. The stage had opened a possibility for another generation of the family.[cite:shakti-aditya-2012]

The family business also moved into another form of work between languages. Aradhana Films developed dubbing and localisation for international films, with the Hindi version of Jurassic Park among its early projects in 1993. This later enterprise continued, in a different form, the work of helping stories travel between audiences.[cite:shakti-studio]

The Notunpalli Durga Puja

His life outside the studio included helping Bengalis in Bombay create a shared cultural gathering. In the early 1970s, residents of Bandra, Khar and Santa Cruz approached him about organising a Durga Puja. The Notunpalli Sarbojanin Durgotsav Committee took shape with businessman Arun Banerjee as its first president and Samanta as chief adviser. The first puja was held in 1972 at Raosaheb Patwardhan Udyan, opposite Bandra’s National College. Pramod Chakraborty, Hrishikesh Mukherjee, Basu Chatterjee, Salil Chowdhury and R D Burman were among the filmmakers and musicians who joined the effort.[cite:shakti-notunpalli-2025]

Ashim recalled that the annual celebration became known as “Shakti Samanta’s Durga Puja”, even though his father chose never to serve as committee president. His association with the festival gave Bengalis living far from home another place to meet and celebrate together.[cite:shakti-notunpalli-2025]

The familiar name outlived him. In 2014, Anandabazar described the Bandra celebration as the puja known by his name. Basu Chatterjee told the Times of India that the festival welcomed people from all communities. Notunpalli became part of Samanta’s contribution to the wider Bengali cultural life of Bombay.[cite:shakti-notunpalli-2014][cite:shakti-notunpalli-2013]

The mentor remembered by his colleagues

Some of the most affectionate accounts of Samanta come from Bengali filmmaker Prabhat Roy. Writing in Anandabazar in January 2025, Roy remembered that Samanta’s birthday brought a month’s bonus for his staff. Good work was praised in front of colleagues. When Roy later needed equipment for his own films, Samanta helped him obtain it at reduced rates. A visit to Bombay also meant an invitation to dinner at his home.[cite:shakti-prabhat-roy-2025]

Roy recalled advice that explains much about the older director’s approach. A film should allow ordinary people to recognise their own lives, or let them see a hero resist the injustices they could not challenge themselves. That principle connects the domestic emotions of Samanta’s dramas with the more forceful action of his later work. For Roy, he was both a teacher of filmmaking and someone who made younger colleagues feel part of a family.[cite:shakti-prabhat-roy-2025]

Later years and a lasting place in film history

His career also included disappointments. By the 1980s and 1990s, several films struggled to reproduce the impact of his earlier successes. His final Hindi directorial film was Geetanjali in 1993, and he returned to Bengali filmmaking with Devdas in 2002. The uneven later record is part of the life of a working filmmaker who kept trying new productions across several decades.[cite:shakti-cinemaazi-profile]

His responsibilities extended beyond his own films. He served several terms as president of the Indian Motion Picture Producers’ Association, chaired the Central Board of Film Certification from April 1991 to June 1998, and later chaired the Satyajit Ray Film and Television Institute in Kolkata during 2001–2004. These positions placed him within the organisations responsible for production interests, film certification and the training of future filmmakers.[cite:shakti-imppa][cite:shakti-cbfc][cite:shakti-srfti]

His productions Aradhana and Anuraag received Filmfare’s Best Film awards at the 1970 and 1974 ceremonies respectively. Recognition of this kind accompanied a more familiar legacy: films and songs that continued to circulate long after their original theatrical runs.[cite:shakti-filmfare]

Shakti Samanta died in Mumbai on 9 April 2009, aged 83. His journey had taken him from Bokra and an early family bereavement through teaching, translation and studio apprenticeship to a career spanning Hindi and Bengali cinema. The breadth of that journey helps explain his films: he understood the attraction of distant places and glamorous stars, while continuing to return to the human need for affection, dignity and a place to belong.[cite:shakti-cinemaazi-profile]



 

Bengali fiction · Editing · Cinema

Ramapada Chowdhury

1922–2018 · Novelist, short-story writer and editor[cite:ramapada-biography]

Born in Kharagpur, Ramapada Chowdhury became a distinctive voice in modern Bengali fiction. He wrote his first story, Tragedy, in 1940.[cite:ramapada-centenary-family] His first novel, Pratham Prahar, appeared in 1954, followed by the wider recognition brought by Banpalashir Padabali in 1960.[cite:ramapada-biography]

Early in his career he wrote under the names Patranabish and Arjun Ray, financing his first three story collections from feature-writing earnings. He later edited both the Sunday supplement and Puja annual of Anandabazar Patrika, encouraging writers including Dibyendu Palit.[cite:ramapada-centenary-family] His long editorial career ran alongside his own novels and short stories.[cite:ramapada-publisher]

His fiction examined ordinary lives, insecurity and social change. In her centenary essay, Tilottama Majumdar emphasises his restrained prose and attention to middle- and lower-income people. She remembers an exacting editor who quietly helped younger writers find confidence.[cite:ramapada-bengali]

He received the Rabindra Puraskar for Ekhoni in 1971 and the Sahitya Akademi Award for Bari Badle Jay in 1988.[cite:ramapada-biography][cite:ramapada-akademi] His stories also reached film audiences: Mrinal Sen adapted Kharij, while Tapan Sinha’s Ek Doctor Ki Maut drew on Abhimanyu.[cite:ramapada-biography]

Publishing · Sanskrit literature · English readership

Pratap Chandra Roy

1842–1895 · Publisher of the English Mahabharata

A publisher of the Mahabharata

Pratap Chandra Roy was a Calcutta bookseller and publisher associated with bringing Indian classics to a wider readership. He undertook the publication of a complete English prose Mahabharata, which appeared in instalments between 1883 and 1896. The enterprise joined Roy’s work as publisher with the translation of Kisari Mohan Ganguli; after Roy’s death, his widow, Sundari Bala Roy, carried the publication to completion.[cite:pratap-sundari-1896][cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript]

Roy built this undertaking through years of bookselling, fundraising and the circulation of religious literature. His own publishing statements reveal the practical scale of the work. The later family biography in Bangsha Parichay adds a personal history of bereavement, education and enterprise, extending the outline preserved in the publication record. Read together, these sources show how a bookseller created an organisation capable of sustaining a large literary project.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

Shanko and the journey to Calcutta

Roy came from Shanko in Burdwan district. Bangsha Parichay names his parents as Ramjay Roy and Drabamayi Debi and gives his birth as 15 March 1842. Its account of his childhood expressly draws on the recollections of his elder sister, Sarbamangala. After their mother’s death, Sarbamangala cared for him; a widowed relative, Krishnamani Debi, subsequently took him to Kalna and brought him up as her son. These details belong to a family history published in 1934, nearly four decades after Roy’s death.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934]

The same account remembers a childhood in which obtaining an education depended on small acts of assistance. After his father’s death, Krishnamani struggled to maintain the household. A Brahmin neighbour provided support in return for her domestic work and the boy’s cattle-herding, before arranging his admission to a local pathshala. One remembered episode describes Roy collecting coconuts offered to the Ganga and selling them to buy schoolbooks. The story gives a concrete form to the family’s memory of his early difficulty gaining access to books.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934]

Bangsha Parichay places his move to Calcutta after Krishnamani’s death. It connects his early employment to Kaliprasanna Singha, the patron of a Bengali Mahabharata, first in household service and later in rent collection. It also recalls his concern for people unable to obtain copies of Singha’s publication. The chapter’s ages and dates do not fit together consistently, so the precise timetable of this employment remains uncertain. The association is best understood as a later account of his introduction to a world where publishing and religious charity met.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934]

His place in Aguri history has a near-contemporary printed reference. In Hindu Castes and Sects (1896), p. 158, Jogendra Nath Bhattacharya identifies Pratap Chandra Roy as a Suta Aguri and praises his enterprise as a publisher. Bhattacharya also distinguishes the publisher from the scholar who performed the translation.[cite:bhattacharya-1896]

Bangsha Parichay situates the family in Shanko’s remembered landscape of moats, temples and tanks. Its claims about distant royal ancestors and Mughal grants belong to family tradition; they do not establish an independently documented pedigree. The more immediate evidence concerns the household from which Roy emerged and the local connections his family maintained after his death.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934]

From a small shop to a publishing institution

The family biography describes Roy beginning business in a small room beside the Seal family’s Jorasanko house, then occupied by the Calcutta Normal School. He sold schoolbooks, including Barnaparichay, arithmetic tables, stationery and inexpensive goods for pupils. The shop grew into a business supplying goods to order. Roy’s own earlier account confirms the broader progression: he had worked as a bookseller and general agent, accumulated savings and reached a position in which he could have retired. He chose instead to invest in publishing Sanskrit religious literature in forms accessible to a wider public.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

His first Bengali Mahabharata was a substantial commercial commitment. Roy states that he printed 3,000 copies, priced a complete set at 42 rupees and took a little more than seven years to finish the edition. Following a domestic bereavement, he travelled outside Calcutta and heard readers praise the work while explaining that its price remained beyond their means. Returning to approximately 1,000 unsold copies, he began giving them away. When some recipients resold their copies, he sought a more organised way of distributing the books.[cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

His publishing work was also philanthropic. He founded the Datavya Bharat Karyalaya to circulate translations of Sanskrit religious literature.[cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

The contemporary accounts point to a charitable programme taking shape around 1876: Roy’s introductory account, issued with the English undertaking in 1883, looks back over seven years of free publication, while Sundari Bala’s completion statement in 1896 describes twenty years of institutional work. This is an approximate chronology of activity, rather than a securely established date of formal foundation. Bangsha Parichay locates the office at 67 Upper Chitpur Road and records its move in 1886 to premises Roy purchased at 1 Raja Gurudas Street.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-publishing-preface][cite:pratap-sundari-1896]

Durgacharan Bandyopadhyay was central to the enterprise. Sundari Bala credits him with Bengali translations of the Mahabharata, Ramayana, Harivamsa and Srimad-Bhagavata, supervision of the Sanskrit Mahabharata, and management of finances and the printing establishment. The institution’s output extended well beyond the English epic. In 1896 she reported four Bengali Mahabharata editions, three Sanskrit editions and other religious works, amounting altogether to 259,000 physical volumes over twenty years. That figure is the publisher’s own aggregate across several works and languages, not a count of complete English Mahabharata sets.[cite:pratap-sundari-1896]

Financing the English edition

The English project was under discussion by 1882. Roy’s preface quotes a letter dated 6 October that year from Reinhold Rost conveying the Marquis of Hartington’s support. Publication began in 1883. Roy hoped that English would carry Indian literature to readers abroad and to educated Indians; he also argued that familiarity with the epic could help British administrators understand the people they governed. His programme combined religious and cultural commitment with an active search for support from colonial officials and Indian patrons.[cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

The distinction between publisher and translator is essential. Kisari Mohan Ganguli translated the English Mahabharata, while Roy organised its publication. Ganguli’s initial anonymity contributed to later editions incorrectly crediting the translation to P. C. Roy.[cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript][cite:mahabharata-publisher]

Ganguli’s own concluding postscript, dated 15 July 1896, explains that he had requested anonymity because so large an undertaking might eventually require more than one translator. Roy opposed that anonymity. They agreed that the publisher would sign his own preface while the translator’s preface remained unsigned. This contemporary explanation gives no basis for depicting Roy as deliberately appropriating Ganguli’s authorship. Ganguli also acknowledged assistance with portions of the work, while describing the translation as substantially his own.[cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript]

The original financial plan estimated a cost of 100,000 rupees and proposed an edition of 1,250 copies. Most were intended for presentation to Indian patrons, officials and scholars abroad, with a reserve for replacement. A further 250 were to be supplied on payment: 50 rupees in India or 65 rupees overseas, including postage, with some concessions for readers unable to pay the full amount. Roy described these paying recipients as donors. The English Mahabharata was therefore a predominantly charitable venture supported partly by paid subscriptions; free circulation did not mean that every copy was free. These figures describe the announced plan, not an audited final allocation.[cite:pratap-publishing-preface]

Production also depended on the timing of support. Bangsha Parichay describes payments arriving months after copies had been delivered and Roy mortgaging his house to meet immediate expenses. Sundari Bala’s contemporary statement confirms that substantial patronage coexisted with debt: she reported about 45,000 rupees from the Secretary of State and British Indian governments, alongside important contributions from Indian rulers, yet approximately 10,000 rupees remained owing when the work was complete. The printing establishment had to keep operating between receipts, with staff, materials and distribution continually requiring money.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-sundari-1896]

The British press and readers abroad

Roy’s enterprise attracted attention in Britain as well as in the English-language press of British India. A review in The Times of London dated 9 October 1885, preserved as a reprint with a Vana Parva fascicle, discussed the first eighteen parts. It drew attention to the scale of the undertaking, its estimated cost and the provision of copies without charge. It also considered the prospects of bringing the epic before European readers, while doubting that it would become as familiar or popular as Homer and Virgil.[cite:pratap-times-1885]

The review’s praise came with an imperial interpretation. It presented the revival of Indian literary activity as evidence of the benefits of British rule. This is the newspaper’s political framing, rather than a neutral explanation of Roy’s achievement. Its discussion nevertheless shows that a Calcutta publishing venture had become a subject for a major London newspaper, with questions of literary value, translation and philanthropy reaching readers far beyond its original Bengali audience.[cite:pratap-times-1885]

Newspaper attention could assist fundraising. In her 1896 postscript, Sundari Bala recalled Max Müller repeatedly bringing the project before readers of the London Times and other journals, attracting interest among scholars and Indian patrons. Her testimony establishes the importance she attached to this publicity; it does not identify Müller as the author of the unsigned review of 9 October 1885.[cite:pratap-sundari-1896][cite:pratap-times-1885]

The English-language press in India formed a related but distinct audience. A Times of India notice dated 19 December 1885 acknowledged that the work and its costs had exceeded Roy’s expectations and supported his appeal for assistance. Earlier notices from the Englishman, Pioneer and other newspapers were collected in the 1884 Adi Parva appendix. These publisher-selected extracts helped promote the project, although some blurred the roles of publisher and translator. They document reception in British India and should not be counted as newspapers published in Britain.[cite:pratap-times-1885][cite:pratap-press-1884]

The response was not uniformly admiring. In “The Epics of India,” published in Lahore’s Civil and Military Gazette on 24 August 1886, Rudyard Kipling discussed the twenty-fifth instalment. He admired its printing and the perseverance behind it, but sharply questioned the epic’s appeal to contemporary readers. His review shows how recognition of the publisher’s labour could coexist with dismissive judgments about the literature itself. Like some other notices, it wrongly associated Roy with the translation, a confusion that the publication’s own prefaces resolve.[cite:pratap-kipling-1886]

A later British tribute associated the completed work with Roy’s death. Bangsha Parichay reproduces a passage from Edwin Arnold’s “A Dead Man’s Victory,” attributed to the Daily Telegraph. Hendrik Clemens Muller’s Lectures on the Science of Literature independently identifies two Daily Telegraph articles under that title in June 1897. The month and year are therefore better supported than an exact day taken from an indistinct later reproduction. This posthumous coverage framed the finished Mahabharata as the fulfilment of a publisher’s commitment that had survived him.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-arnold-1897]

Completion and remembrance

The personal history in Bangsha Parichay gives the publishing story a wider family setting. It names Roy’s first wife as Golap Sundari and their daughter as Haridasi, and describes the grief caused by Golap Sundari’s early death and Haridasi’s later widowhood. Sundari Bala, his second wife, became responsible for carrying his final undertaking forward. These family details come from the later biography; the publication’s own closing statements document her work as its surviving publisher.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934][cite:pratap-sundari-1896]

Roy died in 1895. He had published the first fourteen parvas, or books; Sundari Bala published the remaining four. The English Mahabharata was completed in 1896.[cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript][cite:mahabharata-publisher]

Completion depended on continuing cooperation. Sundari Bala records Ganguli’s promise to finish the translation and Roy’s concern for subscribers who had already paid. She also credits Aghore Nath Banerjee with managing the institution through Roy’s final illness and helping her sustain it afterwards. Her closing statement makes clear that the last instalments emerged from continued labour and financial responsibility after the founder’s death. Ganguli’s dated postscript supplies a firm contemporary anchor for the completion year.[cite:pratap-sundari-1896][cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript]

The family’s remembrance also returned to Shanko. Bangsha Parichay credits Sundari Bala with carrying out provisions for the Golap Sayar tank, named for Golap Sundari, and the Prataperswar Shiva shrine. Haridasi later financed repairs to the temple and the dredging of the tank. These local works connect the internationally circulated books to the family’s continuing obligations in its home village.[cite:pratap-bangsha-1934]

Roy’s achievement rests in the sustained organisation of publication: committing his resources, securing assistance, maintaining a press and finding readers for works on a formidable scale. Ganguli’s translation, the contributions of the Bengali scholars and publishing staff, and Sundari Bala’s completion of the project are all part of that history. Recognising their different responsibilities gives a fuller account of how the English Mahabharata reached its readers.

Business · Literature and scholarship · Public life

Satya Kinkar Sahana

5 May 1874 – 7 October 1960 · Shuripushkarini and Bankura

Satya Kinkar Sahana’s life joined the counting house, the landed estate, scholarship & literature  and the provincial legislature. Born on 5 May 1874 at Shuripushkarini in Bankura, he became a businessman, landlord, writer, regional scholar and public representative. His family’s prosperity connected the agricultural economy of western Bengal with the coal and mica districts of Bihar. His business and landholding interests underpinned his public life. [cite:sahana-charitabhidhan][cite:dutta-2023]

The family’s prominence rested on accumulated capital, property and social connections. These resources supported education, cultural patronage and public service; they also gave Sahana a substantial interest in preserving property rights, commercial security and local authority. His career suggests a form of paternalist reformism: a willingness to improve conditions through schools, medicine, irrigation and scholarship while retaining the hierarchy within which prosperous families exercised leadership. This is an interpretation of his holdings and recorded actions, rather than a claim to know the private motive behind every decision. [cite:dutta-2023][cite:sahana-council-august-1932][cite:sahana-council-january-1934][cite:sahana-council-quinine-irrigation-1934]

A family fortune built across Bengal and Bihar

Achintya Kumar Dutta identifies the Sahanas as an Ugra Kshatriya, or Aguri, family whose activities expanded from cultivation and moneylending into transport, warehousing, mining and rice milling. His account, in Explorations in Colonial Bengal, traces their commercial growth through Raniganj, Giridih and other centres. Railways opened opportunities for moving goods and developing mineral businesses. Profits from commerce and mining were subsequently invested in land. Their caste background located them within the agrarian society of Rarh; their particular class position arose from the scale and variety of their assets. [cite:dutta-2023]

The immediate makers of this fortune included Sahana’s grandfather Gosain Das, his father Prankrishna and his paternal uncle Rajkrishna. The Bengali family chronicle Bangsha-Parichay remembers Gosain Das learning Hindi and account keeping while working for a timber merchant. It describes Prankrishna’s management of family property and trade, and Rajkrishna’s expansion into contracting, mica and coal. The story is one of several generations of business experience, apprenticeship and investment. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

Satya Kinkar assumed responsibility for the joint family’s affairs when his father and uncle died within a short interval in 1908. He continued to invest mining profits in land and settled in Bankura in 1917. Dutta describes his eventual holdings as seven zamindaris and eight houses across Bengal and Bihar, and identifies him as a landlord, moneylender and grain dealer. The same account credits his Sridhar Rice Mill with encouraging other Bengali traders to enter rice milling in Bankura. These are substantial, overlapping interests in production, credit, processing and rent collection. [cite:dutta-2023]

Ananda Kutir, his Bankura home, became a base for family, agricultural and civic activity. The 1932 chronicle describes wells and tanks dug to improve a large stretch of stony land, with fruit, flower and vegetable cultivation. It also records his involvement with the local college and district school, municipal affairs, cooperatives and the honorary magistracy. Such positions brought a prosperous householder into repeated contact with teachers, officials and neighbours. They provided opportunities for useful work and reinforced his standing as a district notable. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

Property, credit and the protection of wealth

His class affiliation becomes especially concrete in the court record. In Sunder Mull v. Satya Kinker Sahana, decided on 6 December 1927, the Privy Council considered a mortgage for Rs 12,000 made in 1907, carrying 15 per cent annual interest and annual compounding of unpaid interest. By the start of the suit in 1919, the judgment recorded Rs 37,000 outstanding. The borrowers challenged the family managers’ authority to accept those terms. The appeal was dismissed and the lenders’ position upheld. The case illustrates the legal machinery through which the family defended its credit interests; the judgment did not declare this loan unlawful. [cite:sahana-privy-council-1927]

The family chronicle gives litigation a humorous face. Sahana reportedly joked that, although he had avoided becoming a lawyer because he disliked lawsuits, he should enter going from case to case as his occupation in the next census. The joke makes sense in a life crowded with property disputes. It also reminds us that the practical management of wealth demanded time, documentation and repeated engagement with colonial courts. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

Historian J. H. Broomfield, using family papers and diaries, describes litigation and mortgage foreclosure as instruments of expansion and links the family’s land accumulation with Santal dispossession. His findings challenge the celebratory family narrative. [cite:sahana-broomfield]

For Sahana, maintaining the status quo therefore had a material meaning. A dependable legal order protected debts, leases and estates. Public standing helped a family act effectively within that order. Support for agricultural improvement or medical care was compatible with these interests: healthier villages and more productive cultivation could benefit tenants and also sustain the economy from which landlords and traders drew income. The evidence supports this connection between class and outlook, without reducing every act of generosity to calculation. [cite:dutta-2023][cite:sahana-privy-council-1927][cite:sahana-council-august-1932][cite:sahana-council-january-1934]

The household: women, children and local obligations

The family’s public reputation was also made through women’s work. Sahana’s mother was Hridaybasini Devi, also known as Ambika Devi, daughter of Durgacharan Ray and granddaughter of Kamalakanta Ray of Kendur. His wife, Binodini Devi, was the daughter of Ashutosh Datta of Paita. Bangsha-Parichay portrays both women as active in caring for neighbours and dependants. Their presence helps explain how the household exercised local patronage in everyday life. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

One of the most revealing stories concerns Ambika and the harvest workers. While Prankrishna was away, she arranged fish and date-palm jaggery for a substantial meal for tenants working on the family’s harvest. Satya, who had recently left college, questioned the need for such an elaborate meal and suggested that it would reduce their afternoon work. His mother rebuked him: [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

“গরীব প্রজারা শুধু কি তোর কাজ করতেই আসে?”

“Do the poor tenants come only to do your work?”

According to the chronicle, he was ashamed and arranged the food. The context matters: these tenants were performing begar, customary unpaid labour. The anecdote records a demand that privilege carry obligations. Its moral world assumes the continuing authority of the landlord’s household, with good treatment bestowed from above. It is evidence of paternalism and a maternal lesson in responsibility, rather than proof of equality on the estate. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

Binodini’s own activities deserve equal attention. The chronicle describes her sending food and provisions to poor neighbours during illness, welcoming local Lohar, Khaira and Bauri families at Bijaya, and joining the Bankura Mahila Samiti. These details show how care, food and sociability helped maintain the family’s relationships beyond its gates. They remain the account of a sympathetic family source, whose praise must be read with that perspective in mind. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

The 1932 genealogy names six sons: Debeshbijoy, Sureshbijoy, Rameshbijoy, Dhaneshbijoy, Raneshbijoy and Pareshbijoy. It names three daughters: Manjumalini, Pankajabasini and Shantibhashini. Broomfield later refers to four daughters, so the 1932 list does not establish a definitive lifetime total. Debeshbijoy managed the rice mill before taking responsibility for mica operations at Koderma; Rameshbijoy subsequently managed the mill. Pareshbijoy later helped preserve and publish his father’s intellectual legacy. This was a household in which business responsibilities, education and cultural work passed between generations. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932][cite:sahana-bibidha-prabandha-1959]

The wider family also combined business with civic standing. Among Rajkrishna’s sons, Kamalakinkar managed mica concerns at Koderma, while Bimalakinkar served as an elected member of Giridih municipality, according to the 1932 account. [cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

A reader who became a writer

Formal education and intellectual appetite did not always move together. Sahana passed the Entrance examination in 1891, the FA in 1894 and the BA in 1898, after illness and examination difficulties. His Calcutta education took him through General Assembly’s Institution and Presidency College. The family biography recalls his borrowing Bengali books from the Chaitanya Library and reading them with greater enthusiasm than he devoted to his prescribed college work. He undertook further studies in English and law without completing those qualifications. [cite:sahana-charitabhidhan][cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

His writing ranged across poetry, classical literature, religion, social observation and regional history. A selection of securely attested titles follows. Bibidha Prabandha gives access to his voice. Its 1959 publication was arranged through Pareshbijoy Sahana of Sridhar Prakashani; the preface identifies earlier appearances in the Bankura periodicals Shankha and Hindubani. They show curiosity about ordinary knowledge, even though his own place in that world was a position of property and authority. [cite:sahana-bibidha-prabandha-1959]

History as manuscripts, places and argument

Sahana’s historical work belongs especially to the study of Rarh and the disputed identities and local associations of the medieval poet Chandidas. Jogeshchandra Ray’s reply in Probasi records that he and Sahana jointly wrote “ছাতনায় চণ্ডীদাস” (“Chandidas at Chhatna”) in 1333 BS, or 1926–27. The surrounding exchange reveals disagreement about manuscript authenticity and the strength of competing regional claims. Sahana participated in that controversy; the contemporary evidence does not justify saying that he conclusively proved Chandidas’s birthplace. [cite:sahana-probasi-chandidas]

Suniti Kumar Chatterji supplies an unusually vivid picture of the work. He recalled receiving assistance and hospitality in Bankura while investigating Chandidas’s songs with Harekrishna Mukhopadhyay. Sahana took them by motor car to Maulban village, where they searched for manuscripts without finding a suitable one. The unsuccessful journey is valuable evidence: regional literary history required travel, introductions, patient enquiries and the possibility of returning empty-handed. Sahana contributed his local knowledge and resources to that shared undertaking. [cite:sahana-probasi-chandidas]

A later Probasi report records his pledge of Rs 1,001 towards the Bankura Sahitya Parishad’s proposed Chandidas Smritimandir. The plan combined commemoration with a library, collections and regional research. The source establishes a pledge and a proposal, rather than confirming completion. Such patronage brought historical scholarship into the institutional life of the district and helped associate the family’s name with its cultural standing. [cite:sahana-probasi-smritimandir]

In the Bengal Legislative Council

The institution in which these pre-independence interventions took place was the Bengal Legislative Council. Contemporary rolls identify Sahana with Bankura East, in the constituency category then officially called “Non-Muhammadan.” Records securely establish his activity in 1932 and 1934. Broomfield describes two Council terms in the 1930s, although his essays differ on the dates: one gives election in 1929 and defeat in January 1937; another gives 1930 and 1936. [cite:sahana-broomfield][cite:sahana-council-august-1932][cite:sahana-council-quinine-irrigation-1934]

On 2 August 1932, Sahana moved a resolution for provincial assistance to Bankura Medical School. He told the Council of a Muslim woman whose difficult delivery had endangered her and her baby; according to his account, Dr Ramgoti Banerjee’s operation saved both. He used this story to argue for affordable rural care and an institution serving people beyond Bankura. The minister cited financial constraints and offered a future assurance. Sahana withdrew the resolution. The record documents advocacy, not a grant secured at that sitting. [cite:sahana-council-august-1932]

The following day, 3 August, he supported a proposal to bring Bengali-speaking border districts under one administration. Drawing on his life in the Bengal–Bihar borderlands, he cited Jhumur and Bhadu songs, devotional poetry, customs and older territorial names. Literary history became political evidence in his speech. His argument also absorbed diverse communities’ practices into a Bengali regional claim, which should be understood as a contested position rather than an objective map of identity. [cite:sahana-council-august-1932]

On 31 January 1934, he questioned quinine distribution in Bankura. How could people in villages far from dispensaries obtain medicine? Could union boards and sanitary staff distribute it more widely? He also challenged the adequacy of the allocation. The government placed part of the responsibility on the district board and did not promise the increase he sought. His questions show practical attention to access, beyond the mere existence of a supply. [cite:sahana-council-quinine-irrigation-1934]

That day he also raised the deterioration of tanks and embankments and asked about irrigation funding. The exchange has a useful corrective: when he suggested that Bankura had no irrigation canals, the government named Salbund and Amjore. A sound account preserves the correction alongside his concern. The result was an intervention on rural infrastructure, not proof that the district lacked canals or that new funds were obtained. [cite:sahana-council-quinine-irrigation-1934]

Reform, authority and the status quo

Sahana’s concern for local welfare coexisted with support for colonial security legislation. On 10 January 1934 he supported the Bengal Smuggling of Arms Bill, presenting it as a response to killings, robberies and insecurity. His speech expressed confidence in government and a desire for public order. This position is consistent with the interests of a businessman and landlord whose activities depended upon stable administration, though the speech’s stated concern for violence should also be taken seriously. [cite:sahana-council-january-1934]

He nevertheless sought safeguards against arbitrary power. On 6 September 1932, during debate on the Bengal Suppression of Terrorist Outrages Bill, he proposed clearer protection from collective fines for inhabitants uninvolved in the specified offences. He warned that local discretion might follow officials’ personal likes and dislikes and deepen distrust. He described his approach as that of a “friend and co-operator” of government. His amendment was defeated. This episode can be read as an attempt to make government more credible by limiting the scope for administrative abuse. [cite:sahana-council-september-1932]

Broomfield’s account records distrust of representative politics, bitterness after electoral defeat, Hindu communal concerns and later support for the Hindu Mahasabha. These positions coexist with the medical-school speech’s inclusive appeal. Local philanthropy, religious commitments, regional pride and defence of social rank could inhabit the same political life. [cite:sahana-broomfield][cite:sahana-council-august-1932]

He favoured improvements that prosperous local leaders could finance, manage or seek from government. Such activity could relieve suffering and expand learning while strengthening the authority of those leaders. Commercial innovation and cultural curiosity could coexist with the desire to preserve property and social rank. His public service and his investment in the existing order formed connected parts of his career. [cite:dutta-2023][cite:sahana-privy-council-1927][cite:sahana-council-august-1932][cite:sahana-council-january-1934][cite:sahana-council-september-1932]

A legacy of learning and power

Sahana remained intellectually active into old age, with several books appearing in the 1950s. He died on 7 October 1960. His legacy includes the writings themselves, his participation in regional historical research, and a record of persistent attention to Bankura’s institutions. It also includes the economic and social relationships through which his family became prominent. [cite:sahana-charitabhidhan][cite:sahana-bibidha-prabandha-1959][cite:sahana-works-bibliography]

The reader who neglected his syllabus for Bengali books, the son chastised over the harvest meal, the manuscript seeker, the creditor and the legislator were the same man. His family’s distinction arose through enterprise, learning and public work within an unequal society. Understanding that society makes his achievements, his commitments and the limits of his reforming outlook more intelligible. [cite:dutta-2023][cite:sahana-privy-council-1927][cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932][cite:sahana-council-august-1932][cite:sahana-probasi-chandidas][cite:sahana-council-september-1932]

Surviving diaries

The National Library, Kolkata, lists 36 of Sahana’s English personal diaries for selected years between 1915 and 1960. Their references are R 30/1–R 30/36, with accession numbers G 24962–G 24997; they were accessioned on 20 December 1973. Freedom movement · Organising · Public service

Shib Shankar Chowdhury

Freedom fighter and social worker of Burdwan

Shib Shankar Chowdhury is named by historian Achintya Kumar Dutta among the Ugra Kshatriya, or Aguri, participants in Bengal’s freedom movement. Bengali accounts also remember him by the familiar name “Kalo” or “Kalo-da”, bringing into view a life of organising and service that continued well beyond independence. [1, p. 314] [6, p. 460]

Family, schooling and the freedom movement

According to the Saheed Shibsankar Seba Samity’s biography, he was born on 10 May 1912 in Bazar village, near Bankapashi in the Katwa subdivision. The youngest son of Rakhaldas Choudhury and Prabhabati Devi, he came from a prosperous family. In 1923 he entered Mathrun M.V. Institution, where poet Kumudranjan Mallick was headmaster. [2]

The Salt Satyagraha of 1930 drew him into direct confrontation with colonial rule. Ekkari Chattopadhyay’s Bengali district history records that he led a contingent from Burdwan towards Pichhabani in Midnapore. Alongside a separate contingent led by Jadabendranath Panja, the volunteers were arrested, sentenced and sent to Dum Dum jail. [6, pp. 441–442]

The Samity records another arrest in Burdwan in 1932, followed by two years’ imprisonment and a 200-rupee fine. His refusal to pay brought another six months. It dates his Communist Party membership to 1938. In 1940 he became Burdwan District Congress secretary at the Barakar conference, an appointment also recorded in Syed Shahedullah’s Bengali memoir. [2] [5, pp. 80–81]

Workers, villages and relief

Shahedullah supplies glimpses of everyday political work. After the 1942 municipal election, he and Chowdhury helped municipal workers petition over their grievances, collecting thumbprints and joining a deputation. His account follows the dispute through a sanitation workers’ strike to a settlement that secured some demands. [5, pp. 94–95]

During the wartime food crisis, Chowdhury and lawyer Musa Mian cycled through much of Katwa and Mangalkot, organising around affordable food supplies. In the 1943 flood relief effort, he worked around Satgachia, Bijur and Kalna; Shahedullah credits him with organising cloth distribution throughout the Kalna area. [5, pp. 112, 136–139]

The memoir also preserves a more personal memory: when Shahedullah was seriously ill, Kalo cycled to fetch Dr Abul Hasnat, bringing him back on his bicycle. As an election organiser, he favoured canvassing from six in the morning, before people left for work. These recollections convey practical habits rather than ceremonial leadership. [5, pp. 238, 280]

Communal peace and later political life

His public work included efforts to prevent communal violence. Chattopadhyay names him among leaders and police whose joint intervention defused tension surrounding a Muslim sweet seller at Mandalgram’s Hat-tala fair in May 1942. The same history records Kalo-da touring Burdwan town and district localities with Communist and Congress leaders to maintain peace after the August 1946 disturbances. [6, pp. 460, 462]

Political activity after independence could still require secrecy. In a memorial essay first published in 1974, Shahedullah names Chowdhury among the district-committee comrades who remained underground in 1951, while others resumed open political work. It is a specific glimpse of his continuing organisational responsibilities. [7]

Death and public remembrance

The Samity’s biography records that Chowdhury was shot dead near B.C. Road in Burdwan on 23 May 1971. Seven years later, on 23 May 1978, the Saheed Shibsankar Seba Samity was founded in his memory, with freedom fighter Fakir Chandra Roy as its first president. [2] [3]

The choice of a health organisation as his memorial gave practical expression to the concern for ordinary people evident in these accounts. The Samity emphasises prevention and health education, alongside services including outpatient care, diagnostics and a blood centre. His name thus remains attached to public service in the district where he worked. [3] [4]

Chemical engineering · Research · Industry

Tuhin Kumar Roy

A life in chemical engineering

1 August 1923 – 4 August 2019

Tuhin Kumar Roy spent his working life bringing chemistry into industry. A researcher, teacher and consulting engineer, he helped develop ways to recover nickel and cobalt from ores and to put Indian technologies into commercial use.[cite:tuhin-roy-1] His career moved between India and the United States and included technical work in Cuba.[cite:tuhin-roy-2]

To understand his contribution, it helps to look beyond the moment of discovery. A chemical reaction that succeeds in a laboratory still has to work reliably in a plant. Materials must move through equipment, unwanted substances must be removed, and the desired product must be recovered. This is the territory in which chemical engineering joins scientific knowledge to everyday production. Roy worked on that demanding passage from an idea to an operating process.

Family and cultural roots

Roy was born in Munghyr, Bihar, on 1 August 1923. His father, Rakhal Raj Roy, practised law in Burdwan.[cite:tuhin-roy-3] Roy belonged to the Aguri community.

Rakhal Raj was also a man of letters, with interests in the history of Burdwan and the preservation of antiquities. His activities connected his legal career with the literary and historical work of the Bangiya Sahitya Parishad.

In 1915, he contributed the Bengali essay বর্তমান বর্দ্ধমান, or “Contemporary Burdwan,” to Sahitya Parishat Patrika. Nagendranath Basu’s introductory notice names him as its author and explains that the essay was published with an account of regional historical enquiries. His writing thus formed part of a shared effort to record and study the region, giving contemporary Burdwan a place alongside its older history.[cite:tuhin-roy-4]

His support for the Parishad extended to its museum. Manomohan Ganguly’s 1922 handbook credits him with presenting a votive stupa from Patna and, jointly with Shivadas Tewari, a sculpture of Varaha, the boar incarnation of Vishnu, from Burdwan. It also records his gift of a moulded brick from Purushottampur depicting Krishna dancing.[cite:tuhin-roy-5]

These were practical contributions to a collection in which religious images and architectural fragments could be brought together for study. The range of his gifts, from a Buddhist stupa to images associated with Vishnu and Krishna, reflects the breadth of that involvement. His influence in cultural life can be seen in these specific acts: contributing to a learned journal and helping build a museum collection. The two generations pursued different fields, but both left records of work that extended beyond private professional life.

From Burdwan to MIT

His education took shape in Burdwan before he studied at St Xavier’s College and the University College of Science in Calcutta. He completed a master’s degree in applied chemistry and worked at the Indian Lac Research Institute in Ranchi. An Indian government scholarship took him to the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1947.[cite:tuhin-roy-2]

At MIT he gained a master’s degree in 1949 and completed his ScD in June 1951, researching fluid-bed reactions under Warren K. Lewis and E. R. Gilliland.[cite:tuhin-roy-3] Fluidization concerns the behaviour of solid particles held in motion by a flowing gas or liquid. It offers engineers a way of bringing materials into contact during processing. Alongside his later work on extracting metals, it remained an important part of Roy’s research.[cite:tuhin-roy-1]

A Bengali and Estonian family

During his American student years Roy met Silva Mardiste at the International Centre in Harvard Square. They married on 1 January 1951.[cite:tuhin-roy-3] Silva was Estonian and had studied at Bennington College in Vermont.[cite:tuhin-roy-6]

A family recollection gives a glimpse of the life they made together. In 1954, after coming from New Jersey to settle in Calcutta, they heard of another Bengali–Estonian couple living on Kid Street. The two families met and became close friends. The account recalls that both marriages had faced family opposition, yet both couples chose to make their homes in India.[cite:tuhin-roy-6]

Their friendship continued as Roy’s work took his family from Calcutta to Delhi and the other family moved to Dehradun. The recollection also remembers Silva’s involvement with nursery schools.[cite:tuhin-roy-6] Such details place a household and a network of friendships beside the more familiar chronology of degrees and appointments. Roy’s journeys were family moves as well as professional ones, and his return to India involved building a shared home across different cultural backgrounds.

Making metals separable

Ores contain mixtures. Dissolving valuable material is only part of the task; unwanted substances still have to be separated. Roy and his collaborator Felix Alfred Schaufelberger developed methods for making these separations more selective.[cite:tuhin-roy-7][cite:tuhin-roy-8]

Their first relevant American patent, filed in June 1954 and granted in December 1955, concerned the recovery of nickel and cobalt from acidic solutions. It described a method of bringing the metals out of solution as sulphides while leaving much of the unwanted dissolved material behind. Small solid particles helped the new precipitate form under controlled conditions. The patent was assigned to Chemical Construction Corporation.[cite:tuhin-roy-7]

Precipitation turns a dissolved substance into a solid that can be collected. The challenge is to recover the desired substances without carrying too much unwanted material with them.[cite:tuhin-roy-7]

A second patent, also filed in June 1954 and granted in March 1957, dealt with separating cobalt, nickel and manganese. It described a sequence in which adjustments to the solution and the treatment of its constituents allowed the metals to be separated in stages. Both patents named Roy and Schaufelberger as inventors.[cite:tuhin-roy-8]

These inventions belong to hydrometallurgy, the extraction and separation of metals using solution-based processes. Extracting a metal from ore and separating it from other metals require related but different operations.[cite:tuhin-roy-7][cite:tuhin-roy-8] He also published “Preparing Nickel and Cobalt Concentrates” in Industrial & Engineering Chemistry in 1961.[cite:tuhin-roy-9]

Teaching and industrial design in India

Roy returned in 1954 to lead chemical engineering at Jadavpur. After another period in the United States and a brief return to Jadavpur, he settled in Delhi, where he helped build the enterprise that became Chemical Metallurgical Design Company.[cite:tuhin-roy-2]

Industrial Consulting Bureau began with S. N. Mazumdar in 1960; it took the CMDC name in 1965. Roy became managing director after returning from further American employment.[cite:tuhin-roy-3] The consultancy provided a setting in which engineering experience could be applied to Indian manufacturing problems.

The Indian National Academy of Engineering particularly recognised his contribution to bringing technologies developed in India into commercial production. His work also extended to coal-processing projects. Beyond his own research, he served as president of the Indian Institute of Chemical Engineers and the National Association of Consulting Engineers.[cite:tuhin-roy-1]

A consultancy occupied a different place from a university laboratory. Its work had to connect the proposed process with the plant that would carry it out. For Roy, teaching and industrial practice remained closely connected parts of a career that repeatedly crossed between the two.

Work in Cuba and the later years

Che Guevara sought Roy’s help with Cuba’s Moabay nickel plant. Banerjee’s obituary describes substantial improvements by Roy’s team and reports eventual operation at 80 per cent of designed capacity.[cite:tuhin-roy-3] S. S. Das Gupta recalled accompanying him to Cuba in March 1967.[cite:tuhin-roy-10]

The assignment brought experience gained in American industry into the effort to restore Cuban production after relations with the United States deteriorated.[cite:tuhin-roy-2] It also shows why an engineer’s influence can reach beyond a laboratory or a university. Keeping an industrial plant working involves technical decisions with consequences for the people and institutions that depend on its output.

Roy became a Fellow of the Indian National Academy of Engineering and served as its honorary treasurer from 1993 to 1995. The Indian Institute of Chemical Engineers honoured him with its Diamond Award in 2007.[cite:tuhin-roy-1] After a stroke in 2008, he continued visiting the institute in a wheelchair and began a book on extracting metals.[cite:tuhin-roy-3]

He died on 4 August 2019, three days after his ninety-sixth birthday.[cite:tuhin-roy-1] His career brought a sustained practical purpose to research: understand a process well enough to make it useful. The teaching, patents and consultancy work were different expressions of that purpose, carried through a life shaped by Bengal and by long professional relationships abroad.

Football · India international · Goalkeeping

Sandip Nandy

Sandip Nandy is a former India international goalkeeper whose career took him from Bengal’s club football to national league championships and the Indian Super League. He played for clubs including Mohun Bagan, East Bengal, Mahindra United, Churchill Brothers and Kerala Blasters, earning individual honours alongside team successes.[cite:sandip-isl][cite:sandip-asean][cite:sandip-ileague]

Family and football in Belenda

Nandy comes from the prominent landowning Nandy family of Belenda, his ancestral village in the Manteswar area of Bardhaman, West Bengal. He belongs to the Aguri community. The family has contributed to the village’s development, including endowing its first school.

His grandfather was a railway superintendent who helped many people from the area find employment in the railways. His father also served as a railway official.

Sandip has continued his connection with Belenda through football. He has mentored a generation of players from the village and surrounding areas and is a role model for aspiring sportspeople in the area.

School-led initiatives in the wider area have also encouraged young footballers. In October 2016, Rajendra Memorial Institution opened a football training school with 40 pupils, drawing children from Belenda and nearby villages. The headteacher hoped the programme would help produce more players like Sandip Nandy. This training programme was organised by the school.[cite:food-belenda-football-2016]

Early years and Kolkata football

Nandy first wanted to play as a striker. His childhood mentor guided his development as a goalkeeper and encouraged his ambition to play for India and the leading clubs. An important early opportunity came with Shonali Shibir in 1997. Facing Mohun Bagan in the Kolkata league, he kept their attack out for 90 minutes before Chima Okerie scored in added time to secure a 1–0 win. Although his team lost, Nandy later remembered the match as the moment he began to attract wider attention. He joined Mohun Bagan in 1999, beginning his professional career in a league-winning side.[cite:sandip-isl]

His move into Kolkata’s leading clubs was followed by success with East Bengal. On 26 July 2003, he was part of the side that defeated Thailand’s BEC Tero Sasana 3–1 in Jakarta to win the ASEAN Club Championship. East Bengal’s opponents had reached the AFC Champions League final that year. Nandy received the tournament’s best goalkeeper award, adding an individual distinction to the team’s victory outside India.[cite:sandip-asean]

Success in the national leagues

Nandy also made his mark with Mahindra United. The club won the National Football League in the 2005–06 season, and he was named the competition’s best goalkeeper in both 2006 and 2007.[cite:sandip-nfl]

Another major achievement followed at Churchill Brothers. The Goan club won the 2012–13 I-League, with Nandy receiving the All India Football Federation’s best goalkeeper award for the season. He later recalled that championship as his fifth national league title.[cite:sandip-ileague][cite:sandip-isl]

A return to the national squad

The 2012–13 season also brought Nandy back into India’s plans. He returned to the national squad after seven years away and was recalled for the February 2013 friendly against Palestine under coach Wim Koevermans. The return gave his season significance beyond the club title. It placed him alongside a younger generation of Indian goalkeepers, several of whom had grown up watching him play.[cite:sandip-return][cite:sandip-team]

In an interview with the All India Football Federation that February, Nandy spoke about the work behind his return. He explained how renewed attention to fitness, after setbacks that included injuries, had helped him regain his place at club level. He emphasised the need to preserve a goalkeeper’s reflexes and remain disciplined in training. Experience, in his account, did not remove the need to keep learning.[cite:sandip-team]

That willingness to learn extended to his younger colleagues. Nandy said he could still pick up things from other goalkeepers rather than treating seniority as a reason to stop improving. The respect ran both ways. Subrata Pal described him as an idol, while Subhashish Roychowdhury remembered asking to be photographed with him as a junior. Their recollections offer a personal glimpse of his standing within Indian goalkeeping.[cite:sandip-team]

Later playing years and coaching

In June 2013, after his title-winning season with Churchill Brothers, Nandy agreed a one-year return to Mohun Bagan. He then joined Kerala Blasters for the inaugural Indian Super League season in 2014. The new competition became another chapter in a playing career that had already spanned the National Football League and the I-League.[cite:sandip-return][cite:sandip-isl]

Nandy also maintained a connection with football in the wider Bardhaman area during his playing years. A September 2017 report described him living in Burdwan and travelling by train to his Kolkata clubs. He continued practising at his childhood coach’s academy in Burdwan, where his commitment set an example for young goalkeepers, according to his mentor.[cite:sandip-burdwan]

By October 2019, Nandy was working as NorthEast United’s goalkeeping coach. In an AIFF interview that month, he assessed India goalkeeper Gurpreet Singh Sandhu as one of the top three goalkeepers in Asia. He had watched Gurpreet at close quarters at East Bengal in 2011 and regarded his subsequent spell with Norway’s Stabaek as a turning point. Nandy encouraged him to seek further experience in a strong overseas league.[cite:sandip-coach]

Nandy’s comments also revealed how he understood the goalkeeper’s role. Discussing Gurpreet’s performance against Qatar, he stressed composure under pressure and the way a goalkeeper’s confidence could spread through the whole team. He praised Gurpreet for avoiding mistakes throughout a demanding 90 minutes and for the assurance he gave the players around him. As a coach, Nandy continued to follow the development of Indian goalkeepers, drawing on his own years in the position.[cite:sandip-coach]
