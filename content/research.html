Aguri heritage offers questions for historians, anthropologists, sociologists and researchers of contemporary West Bengal. These twenty-one research directions connect migration, land and family histories with everyday life, publishing, scientific careers and the experiences of economically disadvantaged households today.

The proposals are starting points for investigation. Their originality, available sources and practical scope must be established through a literature review and preliminary fieldwork. An origin account is a hypothesis to test; community memory is evidence to interpret; neither should determine a conclusion in advance.

Here, economically disadvantaged households means families facing limited resources or insecurity, assessed through land access, livelihood stability, education, housing and debt. It does not identify a hereditary subgroup or rank people within the community. Economic circumstances, lineage identity and social standing should be investigated separately.

Research progress and pending priorities

Reviewed 21 October 2026. The newer articles change the starting point for research. The following work is already available on this site; the remaining tasks concern particular records, comparisons and unanswered questions.

  • Military service and settlement: Military settlers and community formation in Bengal compares several routes from service to settlement and examines the 1767 Burdwan returns. Still pending: named links between service, landholding, households and later community membership; the size and composition of earlier forces. See topic 1 and topic 19.
  • Landholding: the jotedar and dar-patnidar article identifies tenure distinctions and named family examples. Still pending: matching each family to dated deeds and its precise position in the tenure hierarchy. See topic 2 and topic 7.
  • Family histories and early texts: the Bangsha Parichay article and two reading copies make eight Aguri accounts accessible. Still pending: independent proofreading, dated manuscript witnesses, the Sahana grant, the Pal court records and the diaries listed below. See topics 1–3.
  • Recognition and marriage reform: the article on Aguri recognition brings education, public debate and marriage reform together. Still pending: reconciling reported 1920s Jana–Suta marriages with the 1931 census account, and tracing how resolutions affected households. See topic 10 and topic 18.
  • Publishing and scientific lives: the expanded biographies of Pratap Chandra Roy and Manohar Ray now use publishing statements, press notices, referee reports and institutional records. Still pending: the specific archival gaps in topic 20 and topic 21, including the contributions of women and less visible collaborators.
  • Everyday life and contemporary change: Food and Village Life and Aguris Today supply local accounts and dated cases. Still pending: comparative household fieldwork on work, care, migration, marriage and mutual aid. These articles do not replace the surveys and interviews proposed in topics 4–17.

First archival priorities: inspect Hill and Banerjee’s 1940 settlement report and Halder’s 1982 essay; locate the Sahana sanad and the relevant Pal court proceedings; compare the dated manuscript witnesses; and reconcile the marriage evidence. The sections below identify why each is still needed. Publication of an article advances the inquiry without resolving every claim it discusses.

How Burdwan studies discuss the Aguris

Few studies take the Aguris as their only subject. Most of the evidence appears inside larger studies of the Burdwan Raj, rural markets, village religion and rural politics. Reading those studies shows where the community fits in the region’s history, and where it is absent. Each entry below records what a study covers, how it was made, what it contributes, and its limits. A catalogue or abstract is noted as such; it is not treated as a reading of the whole work.

Achintya Kumar Dutta: social mobility and the rice economy

Scope and method: The most focused historical study, published in 2023 as a chapter in Explorations in Colonial Bengal (pp. 297–324), with earlier versions in 1999 and 2011. It draws on census and settlement reports, Bengali histories, community periodicals, newspapers and interviews.[cite:dutta-2023][cite:dutta-lead]

Findings and contribution: Landholding, grain trading, lending, rice milling, education, associations and claims to Kshatriya status together explain local Aguri mobility. Dutta names families and individuals and leaves origins unresolved. His 2012 article on Burdwan’s rice trade supplies the market context.[cite:dutta]

Limits: Many family examples depend on earlier family narratives. Eight Bangsha Parichay entries can now be compared with Dutta’s discussion, but a later historian repeating the same account is not independent corroboration. Dates, grants and ancestral links still need checking against the particular records that support them. Used in History, People and Culture.

Masahiko Togawa: kingship, caste and ritual in Kshiragram

Scope and method: A 1996 social-anthropology PhD at the University of North Bengal, published in 2006 as An Abode of the Goddess. Based on fieldwork at Kshiragram from January 1994 to April 1995, with a household survey, the 1927–28 settlement records, the Raj’s temple-office records and observation of the ritual year.[cite:togawa-2006][cite:togawa-1996]

Findings and contribution: Ugra Kshatriyas formed the largest group and held most of the village’s agricultural land. Togawa counts Jana and Suta Aguri households separately, describes nine title-defined lineages represented in temple ritual, and records the Durga Pujas of five Ugra Kshatriya lineages and a Raychaudhuri lineage chart that credits the Bardhaman Maharaja with the family’s titles. He shows the Datta and Samant lineages holding the Maharaja’s service land and wearing the sacred thread in temple ritual. Togawa also suggests that the Datta lineage’s ritual precedence “might reflect his former status as a regional chief”, a local “little kingdom” predating the Raj (p. 186). It is the most detailed village-level account of Aguri families located so far.

Limits: One village, observed in 1994–95; much of the earlier practice is reconstructed from memory and records; two household totals that should not be combined. Used in History and Culture.

Salini Saha: village deities and farming communities

Scope and method: Ethnographic fieldwork in Purba Bardhaman villages in 2018–2019 for a Presidency University sociology PhD, published in part in 2026.[cite:saha-2026]

Findings and contribution: Identifies Ugra Kshatriyas as Musharu’s dominant farming community and shows them sharing village worship with Sadgops, Goalas, Namasudras and Bagdis. A corrective to accounts that treat Aguri customs in isolation.

Limits: A study of a shared agrarian culture, not of Aguri history. The thesis was read through machine OCR of the repository scans on 9 October 2026; cited passages were read in context, but the OCR is not a verified transcription.[cite:saha-2023]

John R. McLane: the Burdwan Raj

Scope and method: A 1993 Cambridge history of land and kingship in eighteenth-century Bengal, built on revenue and administrative records, Raj farmans and family papers.[cite:mclane]

Findings and contribution: Chapter 6 traces how the Khatri family rose from trade to the kotwal’s office to a zamindari, and argues that Burdwan and the other great eighteenth-century estates were “products of nawabi patronage rather than military adventurism” (p. 125). It dates Krishnaram Rai’s farman to 1695–96 rather than the commonly cited 1689 and shows Burdwan as a Mughal garrison town used by Todar Mal and Man Singh (pp. 128–129, 137). This is the setting for the Nagadee argument, which stresses conquest more than McLane does. Its later chapters, read in October 2026, discuss the Aguris as an old caste of jotdars with “a special relationship with the Burdwan raj” and reject Oldham’s Khatri–Sadgop theory (pp. 157–158), and document the Raj’s troops: unpaid soldiers in revolt in 1760, the battle of 29 December 1760, the 1767 reduction of the “naqdi or household guard” establishment from 1,487 to 839 positions and the 1787 reduction of the zamindari troops to 1,000 (pp. 183–185, 192, 263).

Limits: McLane gives no headcount before the 1761–67 cuts and does not identify Aguri members of the force. The newer military-settlers article checks the published 1767 returns in Long: 648 positions dismissed and 839 proposed for retention, including support roles. These clarify the establishment at that date; they are not a count of Aguri soldiers or an earlier army total. McLane’s evidence for Aguri antiquity rests on the Chandimangal, quoted through Dimock and Inden’s translation rather than a manuscript edition (p. 157).

Arild Engelsen Ruud and Morton Klass: twentieth-century village change

Scope and method: Ruud’s fieldwork in 1992–93 in two adjacent villages of Raina I block, given fictitious names (LSE PhD, 1995; article, 1994; book, 2003), studies how rural communism took hold from the 1960s. Klass’s fieldwork in an Asansol-subdivision village (1970–1975) studies the effects of a nearby factory.[cite:ruud-2003][cite:ruud-1994][cite:klass-1978]

Findings and contribution: Ruud explains the CPI(M)’s rural support through alliances between educated middle peasants and the poor. His second village, Gopinathpur, had an Aguri dominant caste: he gives its population and land shares by caste for 1993 and about 1960, and follows the Aguri Mandal family’s leadership and its alliance with Bagdi leaders from the late 1950s (pp. 16–17, 36–41, 115–116). Both studies show how land, work and status were changing in Burdwan villages, which is the context for twentieth-century Aguri family histories and for figures such as Hare Krishna Konar.

Limits: Ruud’s book has been read for its Aguri passages; his article and thesis, and Klass’s study, only through abstracts and catalogue records. Ruud’s village and personal names are disguised, so his material cannot be joined to named families or places.

Colonial ethnography and gazetteers

Scope and method: Risley (1891), Bhattacharya (1896) and Peterson’s district gazetteer (1910) compiled information from officials, informants and earlier reports.[cite:risley][cite:bhattacharya-1896][cite:peterson-1910]

Findings and contribution: They record subdivisions, titles, occupations and the sacred-thread distinction between Jana and Suta, and they show which origin theories circulated. Peterson repeats Oldham’s Khatri–Sadgop explanation as the community’s own admission, which community authors and later historians reject.

Limits: Their classifications and statements about character belong to the colonial period and are evidence of how the community was described, not of its ancestry.

Sudarshana Bhaumik and Kanchan Mukhopadhyay: caste and regional society

Scope and method: Bhaumik (2023) reads Mangalkavya literature and census material on caste in Bengal from 1700 to 1931. Mukhopadhyay, an anthropologist formerly with the Anthropological Survey of India, studies foodways and society in Rarh Bengal (2026).[cite:bhaumik-2023][cite:mukhopadhyay-2026]

Findings and contribution: Bhaumik places Aguri landholding, literary references and status claims within Bengal’s changing caste hierarchy. Mukhopadhyay lists Aguris among the agricultural communities of undivided Bardhaman.

Limits: Bhaumik repeats a Rajasthan migration tradition more readily than Dutta does, and quotes the Chandimangal line in a different form from Dutta’s, citing a district history rather than an edition; Mukhopadhyay’s Aguri reference is incidental and only a preview has been checked.

What is established and what remains open

Supported by more than one independent source: Aguris were concentrated in Burdwan and neighbouring districts; many were landholders and substantial cultivators; the community was divided into Jana and Suta branches distinguished by marriage and the sacred thread; and the origin of the community is disputed.

Evidence with a limited or shared source base: The named enterprising families can now be studied through Dutta and eight Bangsha Parichay accounts, with attention to where they share a source. Finding a compiled family account is not independent verification of its pedigree. Household, lineage and ritual organisation in Kshiragram still rests mainly on Togawa’s study; the working of Aguri village leadership in Raina on Ruud’s.

Open questions: the size and composition of the Raja’s cash-paid establishment before the 1761–67 reductions, and named evidence connecting Aguri households to its service; the source and scope of the 10,000 Nagadee infantry claim cited by Dutta; and the relationship between recruitment, landholding and community formation. McLane and Chakravarti provide context and pay bills, but the 1767 returns do not settle those earlier questions.[cite:mclane][cite:chakravarti-1970] The force of at least 10,000 in the 1760 report comprised Burdwan, Birbhum and allies together and must be kept separate. See the comparison of military and population figures.

Other questions remain: whether Klass’s village included Aguri households; how Jana and Suta families differed in land and occupation; whether the Datta lineage’s ritual precedence reflects an older local chiefdom or service under the Raj; and how land reform affected the families Dutta names. The newer articles offer comparisons, not new household-level evidence resolving these questions.

Origins, land records and family genealogies

These three connected projects place archival research and fieldwork at the centre. Read the family histories and Aguri origins article alongside military settlement across Bengal, the earlier Nagadee argument, the history of origin traditions and the scholarly reading shelf.

Start from the documents now located

Eight Aguri family accounts have been identified in five Bangsha Parichay volumes dated 1921–1934. The two downloadable reading copies provide working transcriptions and close English translations: one collects seven entries; the other contains the complete Sahana account, with photographs and its family tree. Independent second-reader proofreading remains pending. Follow their references back to the original pages; inclusion in a compilation does not authenticate a pedigree.

The available literary evidence now includes the 1903 Uttarakanda, its manuscript descriptions and the 1897 Patrasayer catalogue notice; Sukumar Sen’s 1955 Chandimangal and its variants; and the 1883, 1911 and 1962 Dharmamangal editions. The twenty-two Aguris reading has a printed precedent in 1883. The next task is to establish the relationship between readings and dated manuscripts, rather than simply to find these printed editions. See the article’s references.

A printed edition, a manuscript catalogue entry, family memory and an independently dated transaction establish different things. None should silently substitute for another.

1. Testing the Nagadee hypothesis of Aguri origins

Research question: Does the surviving evidence support the hypothesis that the Aguri community primarily developed from the Burdwan Raj’s Nagadee forces, or does it support military recruitment from an existing community, several paths of settlement, or another explanation?

Evidence and method: Seek dated military, revenue and settlement records; trace the sources behind later histories; compare literary references, family accounts and alternative migration traditions. Treat “Nagadee” as a military or payment designation to investigate, not an ancestry label. A claim that Aguris formed much of a force does not establish that most Aguris descended from that force. Define what “primarily” would require as evidence, and allow the result to remain unresolved if representative links cannot be recovered.

Evidence now examined: The published government records edited by James Long distinguish the allied force reported in December 1760 from the Burdwan employment returns presented on 28 September 1767. Entries 955 and 958 record 648 dismissed positions and 839 proposed retained positions, giving 1,487 in the two returns together, including support staff. These documents have now been read for the military-settlers article. The next task is to identify recruits and households, recover earlier establishments and trace later livelihoods; neither return supplies a community genealogy.

Competing explanations to test: McLane’s account of nawabi patronage and military contraction, Bhaumik’s connection between the fall of Gopbhum’s Sadgop rulers and Aguri prominence, and Togawa’s suggestion of an older Datta chiefdom should be compared with the same dated records.[cite:mclane][cite:bhaumik-2023][cite:togawa-2006] The family histories also allow migration, local recruitment and established commerce to coexist. These are possible paths to test separately, rather than a single sequence to assume for every family.

Priority documents still to read: Obtain volume I, pp. 20–24 (especially 22–23), of Hill and Banerjee’s 1940 report on the Burdwan settlement of 1927–34, and Ajit Halder’s “Darpane Anya Mukh” in the 1982 Bardhaman Raj College Satabarsa, pp. 51–55. Dutta cites these works; their relevant passages remain unexamined in the research brought together here.[cite:dutta-2023] The British Library record, IOR/V/27/314/35, and Calcutta University’s holding of the centenary volume are retrieval leads, not the texts themselves. Inspect the passages and their own citations before treating troop totals or Aguri recruitment as settled. The 1897 settlement report is a different work.

2. Digitising Burdwan estate records: land grants, tenure and Aguri settlement

Research question: Which surviving estate records establish how particular Aguri families acquired land? How did military service, trade, purchase, inheritance and patronage contribute in different households?

Evidence and method: Begin with a survey of repositories, catalogues, access conditions and surviving record series. Pilot detailed digitisation in a defined period and locality before proposing an estate-wide archive. Where available, connect grant instruments, deeds, rent rolls, settlement records, maps and disputes. Record document references, dates, parties, places, tenure, area and transaction type, retaining original terms beside checked transcriptions. Distinguish a grant from purchase, tenancy, inheritance or another transfer; later possession alone cannot establish a grant. Preserve uncertain names and links rather than silently merging families.

Source lead: The catalogue record for the Burdwan settlement report covering 1891–96, published in 1897, provides one archival starting point. Its catalogue entry does not establish a particular family’s grant; the report and underlying records require examination.[cite:burdwan-settlement-1897]

A named grant to locate: The Sahana entry in Bangsha Parichay XII (1932), p. 83, says that the family’s panja patta or sanad was then preserved. Its present location and authenticity remain unverified. Locate the instrument or an identifiable later copy, record custody and provenance, and examine the date, seal, script, named parties and places. The published report of its survival is a lead, not the recovered Mughal grant.[cite:sahana-bangsha-parichay-1932]

Litigation requires the right record series: The Pal account names lawsuits, but the recovered Board of Revenue indexes for January–June and July–December 1797 and July–December 1854 have not yielded the exact cases. They are revenue subject indexes, not Diwani judgment registers. Identify the court, parties and case dates before searching the appropriate proceedings; an unsuccessful index search does not show that the family’s cases never existed.

Tenure distinctions now clarified: The jotedar and dar-patnidar article identifies named families in the patni–dar-patni economy, while noting that Dutta does not establish every family’s exact deed or tenure level. Match each acquisition to an instrument, date, superior holder and rent obligation. A family could cultivate a jote and hold rent-receiving rights at the same time; neither a title nor a surname establishes its complete property history.[cite:dutta-2023]

3. Migration, land claims and marriage alliances: kul panjikas and oral histories

Research question: How do Aguri genealogies and family memories represent migration, access to land and marriage alliances, and which accounts can be independently corroborated?

Evidence and method: First establish whether accessible kul panjikas, family genealogies and related records survive in the selected sites. Compare versions, authorship, dates, later additions and custodianship with oral histories and independently produced records. Reconstruct a manageable set of family branches and marriage connections, including women’s natal and marital accounts. Record remembered migration as memory unless a historical event can be corroborated. The project should remain viable through oral and documentary histories if no substantial panjika corpus can be located.

Record the teller, date, place and how the story was learned. A remembered commander’s name or compressed generation count may change while a history of service or settlement survives. Test each claim separately; a difficulty in one Pal episode cannot discredit a Sahana, Kundu or Hazra tradition, or unrelated Pal evidence. Conditional generation calculations identify questions, not established lifespans or a verdict on the teller’s honesty.

Methodological reading: Ronald Inden’s study of marriage and rank among Bengali Brahmans and Kayasthas offers a comparison for studying genealogies. It does not establish the availability or contents of Aguri panjikas.[cite:inden-marriage-1976]

Source lead: Togawa reproduces a Raychaudhuri lineage chart from Chaitanyapur, with Bengali-era dates for moves from pargana Dhenya to Eruyar (1154 BS), Kshiragram (1182 BS) and Chaitanyapur (1202 BS), a former title De, and a statement that the Bardhaman Maharaja granted the titles Chaudhuri and Ray Chaudhuri (pp. 147–150). Locating the original chart and any matching Raj sanad would make a strong pilot case.[cite:togawa-2006]

Diaries and the limits of coverage: The National Library catalogue identifies 36 Satya Kinkar Sahana diaries for selected years from 1915 to 1960, references R30/1–36. Their contents have not been examined for this research; the 1932 and 1934 volumes are promising for the setting in which the family accounts were compiled.[cite:sahana-national-library-diaries] Continue the search for Bangsha Parichay XV and XVIII and establish the full publication sequence. Another family entry would broaden coverage, but would not by itself authenticate an earlier ancestor.

Remaining tests of origin accounts

  • Timing: The military-settlers article distinguishes Krishnaram’s death in the 1696 revolt from Jagatram’s assassination in 1702. The political crisis of 1696 is not itself a dated foundation record for the Nagadees. What independent record could establish the formation or reorganisation proposed in the earlier origin argument?
  • Cash wages: Does the evidence behind the cash-army interpretation establish recruits’ previous landholding and residence? Receiving wages does not, by itself, establish landlessness or migration.
  • Land acquisition and later livelihoods: Can named transactions be connected to particular service histories? Compare grants, purchase, inheritance and tenancy with possible Company enlistment, discharge, pension or invalid-grant records. The newer account of Company recruitment does not establish a general refusal of Company employment by settled service families. Test several post-service paths alongside the soldier-to-landholder argument.
  • Rituals and population: Could a shared custom reflect exchange or changing social practice? Would different migration and population assumptions also fit the later counts? Neither ritual resemblance nor a matching projection proves descent.
  • Textual dating: Sen’s Chandimangal apparatus assigns the Aguri variant to manuscript Ma; it cannot automatically inherit the principal witness’s date of 1717. Collate the passage used by Dimock and Inden and quoted by McLane. For the 1903 Uttarakanda, locate the 1009 sal Patrasayer copy and the separate 1502 Shaka witness, photographing the Agari passage and colophons together. The 1897 catalogue notice establishes a source trail, not the age of the verse. Compare the Dharmamangal variants with their manuscript witnesses too; finding the twenty-two reading in an 1883 edition does not date its composition.
  • Patronage or conquest: McLane attributes the Raj’s rise to nawabi patronage; the Origins article stresses conquest. McLane himself notes that where resistance was met, as in Chandrakona and Gopbhum, “the Burdwan zamindari army, and if necessary the nawab’s army” completed the transfer (p. 159). Which dated records of Kirtichand’s and Chitrasen’s campaigns name the troops who fought them?
  • Older chiefdoms: Could lineages such as Kshiragram’s Datta, whose precedence Togawa links to a former regional chief, predate the Raj’s army rather than descend from it?

Heritage, everyday life and missing voices

Starting material now available: Food and Village Life links household food memories, shared ponds, ritual roles and local initiatives. Use these named, dated accounts to design interviews for topics 4–6, recording whose experience each describes. Compare neighbouring communities and household budgets before treating a practice as uniquely Aguri or unchanged over time.

4. Whose heritage? Poorer families, missing histories and a participatory archive

Research question: Which Aguri experiences appear in published histories, family genealogies and public commemorations, and which receive little attention?

Evidence and method: Compare community publications and family collections with oral histories from differently situated households, including poorer and less publicly connected branches. Document work, hardship, food, festivals and neighbourhood life alongside prominent achievements. A participatory archive could preserve recordings, photographs and documents with contributors’ permission and contextual notes. Distinguish a missing record from evidence that someone was deliberately excluded; investigate how preservation, access and selection shaped the surviving collection.

5. Women’s work, care and the transmission of Aguri heritage

Research question: How do women sustain, reinterpret or set aside cultural practices while managing paid work, unpaid care and economic insecurity?

Evidence and method: Combine women’s life histories, voluntary time-use diaries and observation of everyday practices across generations and household circumstances. Follow food traditions, oral narratives, rituals and family memory without assuming that women share one experience or carry sole responsibility for preservation. Compare recognition of this work with its demands on time, income and opportunity.

6. The cost of belonging: marriage, festivals and household insecurity

Research question: How do spending expectations around weddings, festivals and family rituals interact with household resources, social standing and mutual support?

Evidence and method: Study selected events through interviews, observation and voluntary expenditure histories. Compare borrowing, gifts, shared contributions and lower-cost adaptations across household circumstances. Ask who makes spending decisions and how participants understand obligation and dignity. Investigate both burdens and support rather than assuming that cultural participation necessarily causes debt or exclusion.

Land, livelihoods and unequal futures

Starting material now available: Aguris Today brings together migration research, employment and marriage concerns, associational activity and examples of women’s enterprise. Its qualitative cases and public posts provide leads for topics 7–17. They do not measure how common an experience is or establish a community-wide trend.

7. Land, inheritance and contemporary inequality within the Aguri community

Research question: How have inheritance, land division, sales, tenancy and changes in work shaped differences between Aguri households and between branches of the same family?

Evidence and method: Combine household profiles, intergenerational histories and available land documents. Include landless households, tenants and people whose livelihoods no longer depend on agriculture. Compare women’s and men’s reported access to assets and decision-making. Trace documented changes over time without assuming that present wealth or poverty follows directly from an ancestral grant or a community-wide account of prosperity.

8. Education, aspiration and social mobility among Aguri youth

Research question: What enables young people from disadvantaged households to complete education and enter secure employment, and where do their pathways diverge?

Evidence and method: Reconstruct education-to-employment histories among students, graduates and those who left education early. Examine costs, travel, care responsibilities, digital access, guidance and family or community contacts. Compare generations and household circumstances, including gender differences. Following a small cohort over time would help distinguish aspirations from later outcomes.

9. Migration, changing occupations and Aguri identity

Research question: How do migration and occupational change affect livelihoods, relationships with ancestral settlements and the transmission of community identity?

Evidence and method: Link interviews with migrants to relatives in their home settlements, comparing temporary, return and longer-term moves where these occur. Record employment, remittances, care arrangements and participation in family or cultural events. Examine how migrants describe continuity and change without treating departure as either cultural loss or successful mobility in advance.

Marriage, social networks and community institutions

Historical comparison now available: the recognition article discusses marriage payments, Jana–Suta boundaries and reform programmes. Trace particular resolutions and recorded marriages before linking changes in status claims to household decisions or ritual costs.

10. Internal distinctions, marriage choices and changing social standing

Research question: Do locally recognised lineage or subgroup distinctions influence marriage and participation today, and how do they interact with wealth, education and personal preference?

Evidence and method: Compare marriage histories across generations with genealogical entries and participants’ accounts. Separate stated ideals from recorded marriages and present choices. A consent-based network of documented alliances can reveal patterns within the sample, but missing women, poorer branches or unrecorded unions must remain visible as gaps. Do not turn locally reported distinctions into a fixed ranking of the community.

Source leads: The published descriptions of Jana and Suta disagree. Bhaumik describes Janas as agriculturalists who claimed Kulin rank and Suttos as more often professionals (p. 49); Bhattacharya gave the Kulin surname as Chowdry among the Sutas; at Kshiragram the Jana Datta and Samant lineages held the highest places in temple ritual and did not hold the locality Kali Pujas that Suta lineages organised (Togawa, pp. 49, 125, 186). Bhaumik also notes movements to amalgamate the subdivisions (p. 252).[cite:bhaumik-2023][cite:togawa-2006][cite:bhattacharya-1896]

A source disagreement now requiring investigation: Risley’s 1891 account of Jana thread-wearing and Vidyanidhi’s 1896 ritual discussion provide early baselines.[cite:risley][cite:vidyanidhi-1896] The 1931 census report, p. 455, paragraph 486, records different mourning periods and efforts towards unity, while reporting no instance of intermarriage before that census.[cite:panja-census-1931-name] However, Dutta records Jana–Suta marriages in the 1920s (pp. 312–313), discussed in the recognition article.[cite:dutta-2023] Identify the couples, places, dates and sources behind each account and examine their reporting scope. The census statement cannot be treated as proof that no such marriages occurred; neither account establishes a completed community-wide merger.

11. Who helps whom? Caste connections and access to employment

Research question: When do community connections help people find work, training or business opportunities, and which members have limited access to those contacts?

Evidence and method: Combine employment histories with interviews about actual referrals and assistance. Compare people with similar qualifications but different networks, including relationships formed through kinship, neighbourhood, education and previous jobs. Study unsuccessful searches as well as successful referrals. An observed association between contacts and employment should prompt investigation of the mechanism, not an automatic claim that caste caused the outcome.

12. Community organisations, mutual aid and the inclusion of disadvantaged households

Research question: Who participates in community organisations, and how effectively do formal associations and informal networks reach households seeking educational help, emergency support or employment advice?

Evidence and method: Interview organisers, recipients, applicants and people who do not participate. Review available membership and assistance records with permission, alongside accounts of informal help. Compare stated aims with access, awareness, eligibility and outcomes. Include organisations’ heritage activities to examine whether cultural participation and practical support reach the same households.

13. Voice and representation in village life and local politics

Research question: How do caste identity, class, gender, kinship and political connections shape opportunities to raise concerns and influence local decisions?

Evidence and method: Study selected public meetings, local organisations and documented decisions through observation, public records and voluntary interviews. Compare office-holders’ accounts with those of residents who rarely participate. Examine specific decisions about shared resources or services rather than treating a surname, party affiliation or election result as proof of caste representation. Keep participants’ private political views confidential.

Source lead: Ruud’s study of a Raina village with an Aguri dominant caste traces informal leadership from the 1950s: a dispute over the siting of a public hand-pump, an alliance between an Aguri leader and Bagdi leaders that brought Bagdis into the school board and panchayat, and a younger generation’s move to the CPI(M) (pp. 36–41, 84). It offers a historical baseline for a present-day study, though his names are disguised.[cite:ruud-2003]

Where caste-based analysis makes sense in today’s West Bengal

Caste is a useful analytical category when a study can investigate a specific process: marriage boundaries, inherited recognition, social networks, institutional treatment or unequal access. Its relevance should be tested alongside class, gender, generation and location. A finding that caste explains little in a particular setting is also a meaningful result.

14. When does caste matter? Caste, class and unequal life chances

Research question: Which differences in education, work or livelihood security are associated with caste identity, which with economic circumstances, and how do the two interact?

Evidence and method: Choose one principal outcome and combine comparisons within the Aguri community with comparisons across communities in the same study areas. Include both disadvantaged and better-off households rather than equating one community with one class. Report unadjusted and adjusted patterns: land, education and occupation may be part of the historical process, not merely variables to remove. Observational differences alone cannot establish a causal caste effect.

Literature lead: Saha’s study of caste and class in Paarhaati offers a local starting point for the literature review. The abstract consulted for this page should be followed by reading the complete study before adopting its findings or design.[cite:caste-bardhaman-saha-2019]

15. Village, town and city: the changing relevance of caste

Research question: In which domains does caste become more or less consequential as people move between rural, peri-urban and urban settings?

Evidence and method: Compare the same domains—such as marriage, work, housing and association membership—in a small set of linked sites. Where possible, follow migrants and their home communities to connect accounts of change. Separate differences between settings from differences in who migrates; a one-time comparison of unlike populations cannot show that urbanisation itself changed the role of caste.

16. Caste, gender and access to health, welfare and public services

Research question: How do community identity, household resources, gender, documentation, distance and personal connections shape access to a selected service?

Evidence and method: Focus on a defined pathway, such as reaching primary care, applying for educational assistance or obtaining a particular public service. Combine user histories, available records and interviews with service providers. Distinguish lack of awareness, non-application, administrative barriers, cost and reported discriminatory treatment. Compare experiences across and within communities without presuming the reason for an unmet need.

17. Counting caste, missing inequality: the limits of broad social categories

Research question: What becomes visible or disappears when researchers analyse broad administrative categories instead of locally identified communities and differences within them?

Evidence and method: Inspect the definitions, sample sizes and geographic detail of available datasets before using them. In fieldwork, collect voluntary community identification separately from any relevant administrative category and from economic indicators. Compare what different groupings reveal, while protecting small samples. A dataset without an Aguri identifier cannot establish an Aguri-specific estimate; an administrative category must not be assumed from a community name or surname.

Data lead: The PLFS 2023–24 data dictionary provides an example of broad social-group coding that must be checked against the proposed research question. Those categories do not supply an Aguri population frame.[cite:plfs-2023-24]

18. Status claims, reservation recognition and unequal opportunity among Aguris

Research question: Have upper-caste or Kshatriya status narratives and community leadership discouraged or delayed demands for official backward-class recognition, and how have these debates represented the preferences and opportunities of economically disadvantaged Aguri households?

Evidence and method: Treat the proposed connection between status claims and disadvantage as a hypothesis. Reconstruct a dated history from association resolutions, community periodicals, petitions, government orders and relevant administrative or legal decisions. Interview poorer households, women, young people and leaders, including people who disagree or lack information. Compare selected agrarian communities in the Hindi-speaking belt only after documenting their classification and advocacy histories and establishing why the cases are comparable. Do not presume a common motive or outcome from similar occupations or status language.

For education or employment outcomes, distinguish documented eligibility, applications and actual receipt of benefits within the relevant period. Examine costs, information, qualifications, institutions and labour markets alongside caste, class and gender. Community self-identification or a demand for recognition does not itself confer an official category. Central and state lists, dates and applicable rules require separate verification; the official Central OBC notifications gateway is a starting point, not an eligibility finding about Aguris.[cite:ncbc-notifications]

Historical lead: Bhaumik describes campaigns for Kshatriya and Kulin recognition around the early twentieth-century censuses.[cite:bhaumik-2023] Distinguish campaigns to extend or secure recognition of a practice from its first appearance: Risley already records Jana sacred-thread practice in 1891, and Vidyanidhi discusses earlier Jana ritual claims in 1896.[cite:risley][cite:vidyanidhi-1896] The 1931 report records naming and efforts at amalgamation.[cite:panja-census-1931-name] A dated comparison of household practice, association resolutions and official responses is more informative than assigning one adoption date to the whole community.

A specific historical file to retrieve: The Jadabendra Nath Panja biography identifies the 1951 census inventory entry “Caste Aguri”, file 5C-10, and a printed table recording “Ugra Kshatria” as non-backward for census purposes.[cite:panja-census-1951-inventory][cite:panja-census-1951-tables] Locate the actual file and correspondence to examine the reported intervention by Panja. The inventory and table establish an administrative trail, not his personal causal role; neither determines present-day eligibility.

19. From military employment to community membership: a comparison across Bengal

Research question: Under what conditions did service households settle, retain an earlier identity or become recognised within another local community, and what can those cases clarify about Aguri formation?

Evidence already available: Military settlers compares Rajput, Khatri and Mahishya family narratives with the Ugrakshatriya case and the conflicting Itachuna traditions. Its wider account includes Afghan and Portuguese or Indo-Portuguese households. These comparisons establish different kinds of evidence; they do not demonstrate a shared ancestry or a documented admission process.

Pending evidence and method: Choose two or three well-documented family cases. Build a dated sequence of service, residence, land rights, marriage and public identification, using grants, correspondence, registers and contemporary descriptions alongside later genealogies. Seek the earliest explicit community identification and records of kinship or institutional acceptance. Retain missing links as gaps; occupation, district, surname and later population totals cannot substitute for household histories. Include women, dependants and support workers where records permit.

20. Pratap Chandra Roy’s publishing network, women’s work and the circulation of books

Research question: How did the Datavya Bharat Karyalaya finance production and distribution, who sustained its work, and how did the books reach readers in India and abroad?

Evidence already available: The expanded biography distinguishes Roy’s publishing from Ganguli’s translation and documents Sundari Bala’s completion of the enterprise through contemporary prefaces and postscripts. Its British and Indian press material provides a dated reception history. These records are already available; collecting them again is not the main outstanding task.[cite:pratap-publishing-preface][cite:pratap-sundari-1896][cite:pratap-ganguli-postscript]

Pending evidence and method: Locate surviving subscription lists, accounts, correspondence and library accession records to compare planned distribution with actual receipts and readership. Examine the original Daily Telegraph issues for Edwin Arnold’s two June 1897 articles, and establish their exact dates rather than assigning a day from an unclear reproduction.[cite:pratap-arnold-1897] Reconcile the family biography’s inconsistent early chronology with dated records. Study Sundari Bala, Durgacharan Bandyopadhyay, Aghore Nath Banerjee and the press workers as participants with distinct responsibilities. The reported 259,000 physical volumes cover several works and languages; they are not a count of complete English Mahabharata sets or an audited circulation total.

21. Manohar Ray and the institutional history of education and scientific work

Research question: Which educational routes, appointments and scholarly relationships connected a mathematician from Nasigram with Lahore, Agra and international scientific publication?

Evidence already available: The revised biography documents Taylor’s two 1936 referee reports, the 1937 paper, a 1940 Forman Christian College record and the 1960–1970 Agra College principalship. The review and publication sequence is established; the reports’ receipt dates should not become acceptance dates.[cite:manohar-first-review][cite:manohar-royal-society][cite:manohar-paper-1937][cite:manohar-fcc-folio][cite:manohar-college]

Pending evidence and method: Seek university degree and appointment records for Ray’s early education, his Lahore service dates, the publisher’s claim of a Calcutta D.Sc. and his reported Agra University registrarship. If drafts or correspondence survive, compare the submitted and published mathematics to identify the revisions, which the referee reports alone do not establish. Trace textbook editions and documented classroom use without treating current catalogue listings as sales or readership evidence. Compare this career with other documented educational pathways while retaining the limits of an exceptional individual biography.

Turning a topic into a feasible research project

Select one main question, a defined historical period where relevant, and a manageable field area—for example, three to five settlements within one district, with a linked migration destination only if the question needs it. A source survey and pilot interviews should determine the final scope. Findings from selected localities should not be presented as representative of all Aguris or all West Bengal.

Use two kinds of comparison. Compare differently situated families and branches within the community. Add a comparison with other communities in the same localities when the question concerns caste differences. An Aguri-only study can examine internal inequality and community institutions, but cannot by itself establish how Aguris differ from other communities.

Keep a record of evidence and uncertainty. For every historical claim, record whether it appears in a genealogy, is remembered in an interview, has independent documentary support, or remains disputed. Check whether apparently separate accounts copied a common source. A family genealogy may preserve history and express claims to status; record its purpose, authorship, revisions and omissions. Absent women or poorer branches require investigation rather than assumptions about why they are absent. A remembered land grant should remain a reported claim until the transaction can be established.

Preserve sources and protect participants. Retain page or folio references and original images where permission allows; keep uncertain transcription and translation visible. Machine recognition is a finding aid, not a checked reading. Obtain separate permission for participation, recording, identification and public archiving. Use voluntary self-identification rather than inferring caste from surnames. Protect sensitive household information and avoid publishing recognisable small groups or family networks without appropriate consent.

Start with the reading, then return to the records. The source catalogue, community periodicals, historical book and translations offer starting material. Their access and verification notes matter: a later citation of an earlier document does not replace examination of that document, and an interpretation should remain distinguishable from the evidence on which it rests.
