Published 11 October 2026 · Food, memory and everyday life in Bardhaman.

Macher tok and the everyday history of Bardhaman

The history of the Aguris can be approached through the ordinary things people remember: a familiar meal, an annual visit to the village, a festival organised by neighbours. These memories give family and community history a scale that readers can recognise in their own lives.

The Aguris, also known as Ugra Kshatriyas, have a long association with cultivation and landholding in Bardhaman and the surrounding Rarh region. In Kshirgram, anthropologist Masahiko Togawa found them closely involved in the organisation of village worship as well as its agricultural economy.[cite:togawa-1996]

Their cultural history is also part of a wider Bengali history. A dish may be cherished in Aguri households and in neighbouring families belonging to other communities. A village festival may depend on people with different hereditary roles. Looking closely at particular places allows these connections to appear without making every household's customs identical.

Reading the evidenceThe sources range from village ethnography and local reporting to family reminiscence. Where a source explicitly identifies an Aguri community or family, the article says so. Regional memories and the profiles of teachers, musicians and sportspeople are included as part of the surrounding village world; their surnames are not used to infer caste. Local practices should not be treated as customs followed by every Aguri household.

Rice and the farming year

Togawa's account of Kshirgram in the 1990s describes Nabanna as a major occasion for relatives to gather. The new harvest became rice offerings and pithe, or rice cakes. Fish was eaten in the evening, followed by goat meat and visits from relatives the next day. Food also travelled between temple and neighbourhood: representatives of nine Ugra Kshatriya lineages received portions of rice, vegetables and fish on pieces of a broken earthen pot and carried them home to share.[cite:togawa-1996]

Winter brought Pousalla, with food shared beside Kshirdighi, and Poush Sankranti rice cakes. The temple's dedicated rice plot, called pithe-bhumi, joined agriculture directly to worship.[cite:togawa-1996]

The harvest meal makes relationships visible. Relatives gather, offerings travel between households and shrines, and the produce of the fields becomes part of a shared occasion.

Posto and the household kitchen

Posto, the poppy seed used as a paste or in cooked dishes, has a particularly strong place in accounts of the region's food. A 2019 report in The Wall explicitly associated the posto traditions of rural Bardhaman with its Ugra Kshatriya or Aguri population. Its examples ranged from potatoes and ridge gourd cooked with posto to fish preparations, posto paste and fritters. Posto fritters also appeared in the hospitality offered to a visiting son-in-law.[cite:food-posto-2019]

The report was prompted by the rising cost of poppy seeds. Some households were making sesame-paste fritters instead, while caterers continued to serve aloo posto at weddings. This small adjustment belongs in the history of the kitchen too. Familiar tastes have to be maintained within household budgets, and a substitution can reveal how people adapt a custom while retaining an attachment to it.[cite:food-posto-2019]

Macher tok and family memory

Macher tok, or macher ambal, is a sour fish preparation with a place in Bardhaman's family memories. Its seasonings and occasions vary between households.[cite:food-dipanwita-ambal][cite:food-guha-2026]

Writer Dipanwita Roy recalls her family's village near Durgapur, in Bardhaman adjoining Bankura. On Shitala Shashthi, after Saraswati Puja, the household observed arandhan, a day without cooking. Dishes prepared beforehand included vegetables boiled with kolai, dry aloo posto and sour curry made with large fish.[cite:food-dipanwita-ambal]

After the extended Ray household divided its land, its ponds remained shared. Their income supported Durga Puja and worship of the household deity Gadadhar. Around the Shashthi observance, a catch was distributed among family branches. Roy remembers her grandmother frying the fish, then cooking it in tamarind seasoned with panchphoron and dried chilli. It simmered over the dwindling fire before being transferred to stone bowls. She remembers eating it the following day with cold rice.[cite:food-dipanwita-ambal]

Another version survives in Rohitashwa Guha's account of his aunt's marital household in Bardhaman. Pond fish was cleaned in the courtyard; some was fried and eaten at once. The remainder was cooked with mustard tempering, tamarind, mustard paste and cane jaggery. His aunt remembered the tok in a large black stone bowl, served at the end of the meal. The difference from Roy's panchphoron preparation is part of the interest: the name of the dish remained familiar while its flavour belonged to the household.[cite:food-guha-2026]

These accounts also bring the work of the kitchen into view. The remembered taste depended on catching and dividing fish, preparing ingredients and judging the fire. The women who cooked appear as people with skills and decisions of their own. Preserving their names and their methods gives domestic labour a place in the historical record.

Macher tok also has a place in public worship. A 2025 account of Bardhaman's Sarbamangala temple records it among the offerings to the goddess on Ashtami. The dish therefore moves between the household table and the ceremonial meal. Its religious setting is another part of its regional history.[cite:food-sarbamangala-2025]

Food offerings and village relationships

Food could express distinctions between families as well as connections among them. At Rajkusum in Kanksa, members of an explicitly identified Ugra Kshatriya Ray family described their Banakali observance to a reporter in 2023. They collected atap rice and carried it to the Bhattacharya family's Barakali Puja at Gopalpur for the offering of cooked rice, or annabhog. On their return, they brought ritual objects for Banakali worship in the forest the following day.[cite:food-banakali-2023]

The family explained this arrangement through an inherited restriction on its own offering of annabhog. This was a particular local custom, maintained through a relationship between two households. The account shows why a history of religious food must attend to who prepares it, who offers it and how people understand their responsibilities.[cite:food-banakali-2023]

At Kshirgram's Yogadya temple, the ceremony called gua daka similarly gives public recognition to relationships extending beyond one village. In Tarun Chakraborty's 2023 description, a Malakar representative welcomes participants with betel leaf and areca nut. Nasigram's Aguris are specifically among those addressed, alongside communities from other villages. The naming matters: it places the village within a remembered circle of ritual association.[cite:food-gua-daka-2023]

These ceremonies joined people together through recognised positions and obligations. Their shared character existed alongside social distinctions. Both belong in the story of village life; the work of a festival was distributed among people whose roles were understood locally.[cite:food-banakali-2023][cite:food-gua-daka-2023]

Village gods and the life around a shrine

The recollections of visitors add another perspective. Writing in 2018, Jharna Chattopadhyay described Suhari as a village with a large Aguri population and recalled attending its Kalu Ray festival. A symbolic form of the deity travelled by palanquin into surrounding villages. At stops along the route, people put questions to the accompanying devotees, who replied in verse. The exchanges drew on familiar stories and village experience. Worship included the pleasure of listening, challenging and answering.[cite:food-suhari-2018]

Chattopadhyay also named Nanda Pandit, whom she identified as Namasudra, as the priest and daily custodian. Her account makes the participation of different communities visible within an Aguri-majority village. The festival's identity came from the relationships among its participants as well as from the deity they honoured.[cite:food-suhari-2018]

A shrine could also be a place to spend an ordinary afternoon. In a 2019 account of Balgona in Bhatar, Bidhan Ray described a village with a substantial Ugra Kshatriya population where Hanuman was the village deity and the worship space was known as Hanuman-tala. Residents recalled conversation and company in privately owned Durga shrines adjoining the reception rooms of substantial houses. Religious space and the baithakkhana, or sitting room, belonged to an overlapping social life.[cite:food-balgona-2019]

Nasigram provides a further example of how such a space could serve its neighbours. A profile of teacher Soumitra Ray, reproduced on a village blog in 2017, describes his unpaid help to students over many years. He advised them on study, scholarships and opportunities beyond the village. In the afternoons, he sat at the neighbourhood Chandimandap, where students came to seek his guidance. The familiar gathering place had become a setting for educational help.[cite:food-soumitra-2017]

Saraswati Puja in Nasigram

In Nasigram, Saraswati Puja brings these associations between worship, learning and sociability into a large public celebration. In 2021, Junior Club members Nirmal Pal and Rajkumar Ray explained that their organisation in Shibtala Schoolpara had held the puja since 1990. The programme extended over four days, with a fair, bhog on the puja day and a cultural performance that night.[cite:food-junior-club-2021]

That year, the club drew attention with a thirty-foot Saraswati image. Member Soumen Ray explained the decision to make an unusually large idol. This was a contemporary innovation within an established annual celebration, a reminder that festivals acquire new forms through the choices of their organisers.[cite:food-junior-club-2021]

Nasigram Shivgosthi, a separate organisation, described its own four-day celebration in January 2026. Organisers including Subrata Ray and Swarup Hazra spoke of Baul singing, a blood donation camp and changing cultural programmes. People arrived from neighbouring villages and other parts of the district. The organisers presented the puja as an occasion for people to meet and enjoy themselves.[cite:food-shivgosthi-2026]

Belenda and the wider world

Belenda in Manteswar adds music and football to this account of village life. News18 Local18’s March 2024 coverage identifies Bhajahari Das as a shehnai player from the village who had played since childhood. His work gives a name and a life history to a musical practice that can otherwise disappear behind general descriptions of tradition.[cite:food-bhajahari-2024]

Belenda is also the home village of goalkeeper Sandip Nandy, known for his career with major Indian clubs and the national team. Anandabazar Patrika explicitly identified his connection with Belenda in Manteswar in December 2016. In an earlier interview with the Indian Super League, Nandy recalled that he had first wanted to be a striker. He credited his mentor Gautam Shankar with guiding him towards goalkeeping and encouraging his ambition to play at the highest level.[cite:food-belenda-football-2016][cite:food-nandy-2014]

His example had meaning for the surrounding area. The 2016 report described a football training centre at Manteswar's Rajendra Memorial Institution, whose pupils came from Belenda and other nearby villages. Headteacher Subhashis Patra hoped the initiative might produce more players like Nandy. Football linked the school ground to possibilities beyond the district, while the successful player remained a local point of reference.[cite:food-belenda-football-2016]

The teacher at the Chandimandap, the puja organisers and the footballer belong to the same broad history of village opportunity. Their stories show how local connections can support ambitions that extend far beyond the place where they begin.

Food and festivals give this history much of its texture. A grandmother's fish curry can preserve a memory of shared ponds. A portion of new rice can record a relationship between a family and a temple. A gathering may become a place for music, friendship or help with a child's education. In such accounts, Aguri history and the history of its neighbouring communities meet at the level of lived experience. The details are worth keeping because people, places and the work of everyday life remain visible within them.
