Welcome to Aguri Heritage

The Aguris of Bengal

আগুরী · উগ্রক্ষত্রিয়

Rooted in Rarh Bengal, the Aguris, also known as Ugra Kshatriyas, have helped shape a history of courage, enterprise and learning. From Bardhaman and neighbouring Birbhum, Bankura and Hooghly, their story reaches into Bengal’s freedom struggle, its literature and scholarship, its commerce and its scientific life.

The community’s martial traditions recall Rajput ancestry and service in the Burdwan Raj’s cash-paid Nagadee forces. They evoke a turbulent eighteenth century: the devastation of the Bargi raids, local resistance in defence of homes and countryside, and the Raj’s confrontation with the British near Burdwan in December 1760. As the Raj’s army was progressively reduced, later community histories trace a transition from soldiering towards landed influence.These memories of service, settlement and resilience remain an enduring part of Aguri identity.

These memories of service, settlement and resilience remain an enduring part of Aguri identity.In the generations that followed, prosperous Aguri families rose as substantial cultivators, traders and landholders, acquiring interests in Bengal’s patni and dar-patni tenures. 

Their enterprise extended from fields and grain markets into rice mills, commerce and mining. Satya Kinkar Sahana gave that inheritance a distinctive expression through literature, historical scholarship and service to his region.

Learning opened further horizons. Pratap Chandra Roy’s publishing enterprise brought English translation of the Mahabharata to readers across the world. The Ugra Kshatriya Samiti and its journal encouraged education and social debate; From Nasigram, mathematician Manohar Ray carried his research into the Royal Society’s journal. Ganapati Panja combined medical achievement with a lasting commitment to education in his ancestral village.

The freedom struggle gave this heritage another powerful expression. Jadabendra Nath Panja, Dasarathi Tah, Benoy Choudhury and Hare Krishna Konar brought conviction and sacrifice to the struggle against colonial rule. Their paths led through village organisation, imprisonment, public leadership and the defence of cultivators’ rights. From a community with a strong landed presence emerged leaders who helped transform Bengal’s agrarian order.
