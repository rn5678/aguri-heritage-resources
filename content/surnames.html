Words, titles and family histories

What an Aguri surname can tell us

An Aguri surname can preserve a royal compliment, a remembered office, a personal name or a connection with a place. Its meaning is worth recovering. The fuller history emerges when that word is connected with the people who used it, the records they left and the stories their families preserved.

These names reveal different paths through Bengal’s languages and institutions. This article brings their explanations together while distinguishing a word’s meaning from the history of a particular family.

Three questions behind every name

What does the word mean? Dictionaries and historical linguistics can explain a form such as pāla, protector, or trace a Bengali name to an older word. Who used it? A dated biography, register or ethnography can locate a surname in a community. How did this family acquire it? A grant, a lineage chart or an attributed family narrative may answer that more particular question.

These kinds of evidence complement each other. Some names have published linguistic explanations; others have recorded stories of adoption or changing titles. Still others are securely recorded among Aguris while their derivations remain uncertain. We can describe each at its proper level of confidence without treating uncertainty as an absence of history.

Names travel across communitiesA surname can be inherited, adopted or changed, and the same word can enter more than one family independently. The 27 entries below are a guide to recorded names and their interpretations, not an exhaustive list or a test of community membership.

Names, records and interpretations: the fuller account

Aguri surnames belong to several histories at once: the development of Bengali, the language of authority, the movement of families and the memory of local standing. A village headman’s title, a devotional personal name, an honorific and a family’s decision to replace an older designation each reveal a different part of that history.

A word, a title and a family name

Bengali padabi can mean a title or a surname; upadhi denotes a title or honorific. A kula is a family or lineage, while kulin describes recognised standing among lineages. A gotra is a conventionally recognised descent group, often traced to a sage, with implications for marriage. A gāñi can identify a lineage through a village. These categories may interact, but they answer different questions about belonging.[cite:risley][cite:chatterji-odbl]

An office or compliment could become an inherited name. It could also be assumed, modified or dropped, so the title’s literal meaning need not describe every bearer’s occupation. Risley’s 1891 discussion of Chandra even records a title-group in which marriage within the title had become forbidden. Naming could help organise social relationships as well as describe them.[cite:risley]

Several routes into Bengali surnames

Rank, authority and honour. Konar-related forms and Santra preserve older language associated with princes and chiefs. Chatterji derives Koṅgar / Kõar from kumāra and Sā̃tṛā from sāmanta-rāja. Samanta is glossed as a chief in Risley. Such meanings help us understand the prestige of a name without turning every bearer into a ruler. Sahana’s family narrative supplies a more specific example of honour reportedly bestowed on an individual.[cite:chatterji-odbl][cite:risley-vol2][cite:sahana-title-1932]

Service and administration. Mandal and Chaudhuri circulated as titles associated with local authority. Das’s dictionary connects Hazra with the Persian-derived hazār / hazārī vocabulary and records both military command and administrative senses. That is stronger evidence than a resemblance between words, while a named ancestor’s actual appointment remains a separate matter. Bengal’s service vocabulary crossed communities and could become hereditary.[cite:risley][cite:risley-vol2][cite:das-hazra-1916]

Personal names and auspicious meanings. Datta carries the Sanskrit sense “given”; Nandi is explained through a personal name associated with joy; Basu belongs to the semantic range of vasu. Shyam and Yash likewise belong to a different naming vocabulary from an administrative office. These strands make an exclusively military explanation of the entire surname collection inadequate.[cite:risley][cite:ancestry-nandi][cite:dafn-basu]

Places and sound change. Village affiliations could become lineage names; Chatterji explains Pākrāsi through a place name. He traces De to deva (vol. I, p. 303), and discusses the family-forming suffix -jā from jāta. His derivations concern the historical development of forms. A familiar modern English spelling can hide older Bengali distinctions, so the original spelling is part of the evidence.[cite:chatterji-odbl]

The titles recorded for the Aguri community

The sources record Aguri surnames at different dates and with different purposes. Some list Kulin families, some distinguish subdivisions, and others discuss particular households. Their lists differ, but differences in scope do not necessarily amount to disagreement.

Risley, in 1891, described the Aguri Kulin families as distinguished by four titles — Kesh, Pai, Shyam and Pāl — and recorded seven subdivisions, including Jana, Suta and Satsaikiya. He added that the marriage sections bore names such as Kashyap, Sandilya and Bharadwaja which “appear to show that they have been borrowed from the Brahmans”. That is his interpretation, not a traced adoption.[cite:risley]

The second volume of the same glossary records further Aguri titles in its own entries: Samanta, “a chief”; Malla, “wrestler”; Pan; and Rana. It also notes a sub-caste, Shahi Ugrakhatri, “bearing the title Shah”, and lists Sandilya as a gotra shared by the Aguri, Baidya, Brahman, Kamar and Subarnabanik castes.[cite:risley-vol2]

Jogendra Nath Bhattacharya’s chapter of 1896 gives a fuller list of surnames in use. He records Chowdry as “the surname of the Kulins, or the noblest families among the Aguris”, then Santra, Panja, Ta, Hati, Ghosh, Bose, Dutta, Hajra, Kower and Samanta for other Suta Aguris, and notes that “the surname of the Jana Aguris is usually Jana”, while many Jana families bear the same surnames as the Sutas.[cite:bhattacharya-1896] Sudarshana Bhaumik lists Ray, Mandal, Samanta, Chaudhuri and Pal as titles borne by Aguri landholders.[cite:bhaumik-2023] Achintya Kumar Dutta names the families he studied: the Panjas of Sathinandi, the Tahs of Palitpur, the Roy Chowdhurys of Chaitanyapur and the Sahanas of Bankura, along with individuals named Kesh, Mondal, Samanta and Koner.[cite:dutta-2023]

An earlier caste handbook gives yet another account of rank. Lalmohan Vidyanidhi’s Sambandha Nirnaya (second edition, 1896) records that among the Ugra Kshatriyas “Hazra and Chaudhuri are mostly Kulin” (হাজরা ও চৌধুরীই প্রায় কুলীন), and adds that Kulin rank could be gained “with wealth and good deeds”. He confirms the division into Suta and Jana, notes that the Janas take the sacred thread at marriage by rites that “are not Vedic”, and says that eight houses in eight parganas of Burdwan were especially renowned, quoting a verse from the kulagrantha of the genealogist Shashthidas Bhattacharya (pp. 187–188).[cite:vidyanidhi-1896] His account links recognised standing to wealth and conduct as well as inherited names; it should be read alongside the other contemporary lists.

Haricharan Bandhu’s Rajput O Ugrakshatriya names a different pair again on printed page 50 — Yash and Datta (যশ ও দত্ত) — and quotes a kulaparichay verse listing lineage names, among them Dām, Datta and De. He attributes the verse to the kulagranthas of Shashthidas Bhattacharya and Ganesh Kulacharya, as quoted by Panchanan Tarkaratna and by Vidyanidhi; neither genealogist’s book has been located. Read with Vidyanidhi’s note, the verse is a list of houses by pargana rather than of ancestors: Bandhu himself identifies its Mujafar and Barbak as the parganas of Muzaffarshahi and Barbaksingh on the Ajay near Tekurgarh (p. 133), and Satsaika, Dhenya, Bardhaman and Eruyar are also recognisable. Two lines, and two of the eight places, remain uncertain because the printed versions differ. The copy held by this project is the second edition, dated 1940 by the publisher’s note of 27 Baishakh 1347; its preface places the first edition roughly thirty years earlier. Bandhu was arguing a case for the community’s Kshatriya standing, and his identifications belong to that argument rather than standing as independent evidence for it.[cite:rajput-1940]

Fieldwork shows the same titles working as lineage names. At Kshiragram in 1994–95, Masahiko Togawa found the Ugra Kshatriyas organised in nine lineages known by their titles: Datta, Samant, Malla, Sani, Ray, Josh, Chota-Ray, Chota-Samant and Chaudhuri. The Datta and Samant lineages were Jana Aguri, the rest Suta Aguri, and most lineages lived in a para named after them (pp. 49, 145). Josh and Datta are the pair Bandhu named. Togawa also shows titles changing: the village’s Ray lineages began to call themselves Chaudhuri just after Independence, while temple priests still call them Bara-Ray and Chota-Ray. A Raychaudhuri lineage chart gives the family’s earlier title as De and credits the Bardhaman Maharaja with granting the titles Chaudhuri and Ray Chaudhuri (pp. 147–149). The chart places that De family in pargana Dhenya and records its move to Eruyar, the two places the kulaji verse pairs with the Dām–Datta–De houses and the Sen house.[cite:togawa-2006] In Arild Engelsen Ruud’s Raina study village the leading Aguri family was a Mandal family.[cite:ruud-2003]

The same surname in many communities

The shared use of a surname is itself historically informative. It shows that names circulated within a wider Bengali society; it does not tell us whether two particular households shared ancestors. Risley’s glossary, later scholarship and family accounts record overlapping names across communities.

Two entries make the point plainly. Datta appears in Risley’s first volume as a family title of ten different communities. Samanta appears in his second as a title of Aguris, Brahmans, Khandaits, Rajputs, Sadgops, Telis “and some other castes”.[cite:risley][cite:risley-vol2] Lokeshwar Basu makes the same observation in his Bengali book on the history of Bengali surnames: many names occur across community boundaries, so a name is best studied alongside places, dated records and family accounts.[cite:basu-surnames]

Lal Behari Day’s Govinda Sámanta (1874), republished as Bengal Peasant Life in 1878, provides an unusually direct literary example. Its Aguri identification comes from the text itself. These passages place surnames within a literary portrayal of household life, education and marriage. They document the novel’s representation of Aguris; they do not establish a real family’s pedigree, the date a surname arose, or descent from a ruling dynasty. Nor does Madhu Sinha’s employment show that the surname originated in clerical work.[cite:day-peasant-life]

For a short-story comparison, Banaphul’s Sripati Samanta (শ্রীপতি সামন্ত) uses the same surname. A contemporary review identifies the story, but does not establish the character’s caste; the original story has not been checked here. It therefore belongs among shared-name examples, not verified Aguri characters.[cite:banaphul-review] No inspected literary passage has yet been found in this research that explicitly identifies a Hati or Nandi character as Aguri. That gap remains open.

What names reveal about Aguri history

Read together, these names suggest participation in several overlapping worlds: cultivation and landholding, regional administration, armed service, commerce, religious culture and patronage. Dutta documents Ugra Kshatriyas in the Burdwan Raj’s cash-paid nagdee force and the later importance of landholding. Bhattacharya placed Aguris in his book’s “Military Castes” section. These are relevant contexts, while the Sahana account foregrounds trade and public works in its explanation of honour.[cite:dutta-2023][cite:bhattacharya-1896][cite:sahana-title-1932]

Togawa’s account of De, Ray and Chaudhuri is especially revealing: a lineage can have more than one name over time, and a ritual designation can survive after the public surname changes. The Sahana chronicle remembers another replacement, Konar by Sahana. Together they make changing family titles a subject of historical investigation in its own right.[cite:togawa-2006][cite:sahana-title-1932]

Samanta, Rana, Pal, Mandal and Chaudhuri also occur outside the Aguri community. Their presence supports an interpretation of shared regional institutions and naming practices. These sources contain no comparative measurement showing that Aguri surnames are more heavily military or aristocratic than their neighbours’. Nor can a collection of title meanings establish a single migration or common royal founder.

The genealogical verses discussed above place houses in named parganas. That geographic memory can be read alongside service and landholding, while the absence of dates prevents a firm chronology of the houses’ formation. Vidyanidhi’s emphasis on wealth and good deeds also reminds us that recognised standing could be negotiated.[cite:vidyanidhi-1896][cite:rajput-1940]

How to weigh the explanations

A linguistic explanation, a lineage tradition and a contemporary record have different strengths. A dictionary can establish a word’s meaning; a family chronicle can preserve an account of how a title was acquired; an authenticated grant can provide evidence of a particular award. Each answers a different question. A recorded family tradition deserves attention, while claims about ancestry or the circumstances of a grant require evidence of their own.

The same care applies to printed authorities. Colonial ethnographers recorded useful names and practices through the classifications and assumptions of their period. Community histories often argued for status or preserved family prestige. Neither perspective is automatically decisive. Risley’s own admission that he could not verify the supposed founding office-holder behind the titular groups he discussed is a useful caution; his further conjecture that names were adopted at random is also an interpretation.[cite:risley]

The varying Kulin lists illustrate the problem. Risley names Kesh, Pai, Shyam and Pal; Bhattacharya highlights Chowdhury; Vidyanidhi names Hazra and Chaudhuri as mostly Kulin; Bandhu discusses Yash and Datta. They differ in date, scope and purpose. The additional Samanta, Malla, Pan and Rana entries in Risley establish recorded usage rather than a uniform rank.[cite:risley][cite:risley-vol2][cite:bhattacharya-1896][cite:vidyanidhi-1896][cite:rajput-1940]

Some derivations remain less clear, including Kesh, Pai and Tah. A documented surname can remain historically important while its word origin is unresolved. Likewise, a general surname dictionary’s community label is not an exhaustive survey of users: the wider evidence for Basu, Ghosh, De and other names must be retained. Specific uncertainties belong beside the relevant claim, so readers can see what is known and where interpretation begins.

Using a surname in family research

The richest family histories connect a name with a place, a date and a sequence of people. A remembered meaning can guide that work: Sahana directs attention to patronage and a reported grant; changing De, Ray and Chaudhuri titles direct attention to lineage charts and local records.

Useful evidence includes the village, the tenure, school registers, land records, printed notices and the family’s own account of where it came from and when it moved. A name shared with a well-known family proves nothing by itself. An apparent absence also needs context: spellings varied, titles changed and surviving records can be incomplete. Original Bengali spellings are worth comparing before any etymology is proposed, because English transliterations merge names that were never the same.
