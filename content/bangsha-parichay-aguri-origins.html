The strongest historical explanation is that the Aguris took shape as a community in Rarh, especially the Burdwan region, through a long association with cultivation, landholding, armed service and local authority. Some families may also preserve memories of migration and service under Mughal commanders. The evidence does not establish one migrating army, one royal ancestor or one moment of origin for the entire community. It points more convincingly to several family histories becoming part of a shared regional identity. This is a historical reconstruction, with greater confidence in the regional setting and occupational history than in the earliest stages of formation. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8][10]

That conclusion gives family memory an important place. An ancestor remembered as a soldier may have served a local chief rather than the emperor personally. A story of a grant may preserve the establishment of a household even when the ruler's name has shifted. A pedigree may leave out generations without its keepers intending to deceive. The task is to understand what a tradition carries, and then determine which parts can be independently dated. A mistaken date in one Pal episode cannot discredit a Sahana tradition, a Kundu migration memory or the Hazras' village rituals.

Listening to the families

Bangsha Parichay volumes collected family histories, genealogies and biographies from materials supplied by families, within Bengal’s older tradition of preserving pedigrees through kulapanjikas and inherited memories. We regard them as a rich source of oral history, preserving family recollections, migration stories and local traditions that might otherwise have disappeared. Their accounts of marriages, occupations and public life are valuable, although they also reflect how families wished to be remembered. Claims of ancient ancestry or elevated status need independent corroboration, and not every entry derives from an old genealogical manuscript. Read critically, these volumes illuminate both the past and how families remembered it. [19]

The Bangsha Parichay brings together eight identified Aguri family accounts in five volumes published between 1921 and 1934. They concern landed, commercial and professional households. They are an unusually rich window into those families, although they cannot represent every Aguri household, especially poorer cultivators, labouring families or women's experiences beyond the roles the compilers chose to describe. This sample comes from searches across seventeen volumes; two gaps in the numbered sequence and the possibility of later volumes mean that other family accounts may remain to be identified. [1]

These accounts contain several kinds of knowledge. A recollection of a father's business is different from a tradition about a seventeenth-century founder; both differ from an argument linking a family to a medieval dynasty. They should be evaluated separately, even when they stand on the same page. The Pals' claim to descent from the Pala emperors requires a chain of evidence that has not been supplied. Their accounts of nineteenth-century marriages, businesses and village obligations pose different, more specific questions. [1]

The same distinction applies to sources outside the community. A census officer could accurately report a petition while repeating an untested explanation of origins. Risley and Bhattacharya recorded useful details, but also wrote through caste hierarchies, received classifications and stereotypes. Their printed authority does not turn speculation about parentage or character into population history. The community's traditions and the officials' descriptions both need to be read in context. Agra and Rarh can belong to the same history

The family accounts offer several explanations of origin. Some remember arrival from the Agra region with Mughal forces. Others describe local houses entering imperial service. These explanations conflict if each is made the exclusive origin of every Aguri. They need not conflict as histories of different families, or as memories of different stages in the formation of one community. [1]

An imperial army could bring men into a region and recruit others already living there. Service, marriage, patronage and settlement could then connect households with different earlier backgrounds. The surviving evidence does not demonstrate that this exact sequence created the Aguris, but it offers a historically plausible way to read the traditions together without forcing every family into one pedigree.

The concentration of Aguris in Burdwan is strong evidence of regional rootedness by the census period. It is weaker evidence about where every ancestor came from centuries earlier. Soldiers settled in a particular estate could remain concentrated there. Surnames could also change as offices and local titles became hereditary. Geography and surnames make a single, community-wide Agra migration harder to establish; they do not rule out migrant ancestry in particular households. [2][6][7][8]

The derivation of Agari from Agra should therefore remain an explanation preserved by the community, rather than a demonstrated linguistic fact. Similarly, connecting Agari to agrahara, a type of land grant, requires dated intermediate forms and identifiable holders. Neither a resemblance between words nor an ancient grant to people with later-familiar surnames supplies the missing genealogical link.

A third explanation identifies Aguris with the Ugra category in Sanskrit accounts of social rank and parentage. Such classifications tell us how authors arranged society within a normative scheme; they do not by themselves document the marriages from which a particular historical community arose. Later writers also invoke the Brihaddharma Purana, but a passage there naming the historically specific Aguri community has not been independently verified for this account. The connection remains a textual question to investigate, not a biological conclusion to inherit from older scholarship. [6][7][17]

The traditions concerning Man Singh also depend on careful identification of places. The Pal account specifically describes a second battle of Mogalmari. Blochmann's historical note distinguishes the famous battlefield associated with Tukaroi in 1575 from another Mughulmari in Burdwan on the route towards Jahanabad and Mandaran. The family narrative cannot be identified with the 1575 battle solely because the place names resemble one another. No independent record of the family's particular second battle has been identified. A chronological objection based solely on the 1575 battle would therefore be premature. [1][9]

Land and armed service were closely connected

Across these families, landholding and armed service repeatedly appear together. The Ray account remembers a fortified settlement, guards and named military places. The Bilwagram Hazras connect a founder to service and village establishment. The Sahana narrative links movement and settlement to regional rulers, while later generations prosper through trade. These recurrences deserve attention, although shared editorial language must not be counted as independent corroboration. [1]

The best-supported interpretation is that martial and agrarian roles could coexist. A family might cultivate, collect revenue, maintain retainers, protect routes and serve a larger power at different moments. “Warrior” and “cultivator” are not mutually exclusive descriptions of a household's past. Nor does later commercial success require that every family began as a dispossessed ruler.

Modern research places Aguris among the Burdwan Raj's cash-paid troops, the nagdees or naqdis. This supports the importance of military service in the community's history. It does not establish that the army created the whole caste. Achintya Kumar Dutta explicitly rejects an exclusively eighteenth-century origin and argues for earlier presence. McLane likewise treats Aguris as an existing regional element in the Raj's success. The army is best understood as one institution through which some Aguris served and advanced, rather than a proven common ancestor of all Aguris. [2][10]

This military interpretation follows Dutta and McLane. Dutta's supporting sources include Ajit Halder's essay and the 1940 Burdwan settlement report, whose relevant passages have not been independently examined for this account. Exact troop totals, recruitment patterns and the proportion of Aguris in the force therefore require further verification. [15][16]

What each family can tell us

The Sahanas and a remembered trading world

Durlabh Konar's story centres on pack animals, commodities, routes and water. Even if ten thousand cattle is a conventional expression of great wealth, the occupational world described is specific: trade between the Bengal coast and the interior required animals, places to halt, access to water and negotiation with authority. The remembered request for rights to tanks and camping places fits that world. It may preserve an economic relationship more reliably than the name or date of the governor. [1]

The story also combines enterprise with privilege. Refusing one jagir while receiving rent-free tank land does not mean that the fortune was independent of land rights. Kuraram's later settlement on land granted by the Malla ruler adds another connection between commerce and local authority. The Sahana history is better read as the interaction of trading resources, settlement and patronage than as a simple choice between merchant and landlord origins.

The reported panja patta would be exceptionally valuable if it survives. The 1932 entry says that the sanad was then still preserved; its present location and authenticity remain unverified. Its date, seal, language, place names and terms could test the tradition directly. An unspecified gap between Durlabh and Kuraram cannot date the grant to one Mughal campaign. For the later family, the National Library's catalogue confirms thirty-six diaries by Satya Kinkar Sahana, covering selected years between 1915 and 1960. The volumes for 1932 and 1934 are particularly relevant to the world in which the lineage histories were published. This establishes the diaries as a catalogued collection; their contents have not been examined for this account. [1][13]

The Kundus and two memories of the Maratha years

The 1921 Kundu account remembers a family defending the Burdwan Raj's interests during Maratha incursions; the modern heritage narrative remembers Maratha incomers who settled and prospered. Both connect the household to the same disruptive period, but assign it different roles. Their disagreement calls for inquiry into how the accounts were transmitted, rather than an immediate choice of the more attractive story. [1][18]

The pedigree produces a chronological tension with a foundation in the 1740s if all the stated links and the assumed generation lengths are accepted. It does not independently establish an arrival date. A temple inscription or rent-free grant could identify a patron and a dated act of establishment, but even a securely dated building would not necessarily date the family's first arrival. Later marriage links with other Aguri households are relevant to the family's community position by the period recorded; they do not settle its distant ancestry.

The Hazras of Bilwagram and relationships preserved in ritual

The exchange of lamps and festival visits between Bilwagram and Satinandi is a different kind of evidence from a royal pedigree. If its continuity is independently documented, it may preserve a durable relationship between settlements even when the explanatory genealogy has become compressed. The remembered tanks, deities and branches of the Sen house offer several connected lines of inquiry. [1]

The assertion that Hemanta Sen held an imperial mansab of ten thousand is much harder to accept literally. Such a rank was extraordinary in the period claimed. Yet replacing it with a supposedly proven rank of one thousand merely because Hazra is associated with hazari creates another unsupported assertion. The reasonable interpretation is that the title may preserve a memory of office or service; the precise rank awaits contemporary evidence. The difficulty with this number does not invalidate the village relationship or the family's later commercial history. [9]

The Hazras of Kurchi and the value of an admitted gap

The Kurchi genealogy explicitly leaves several generations unknown. This admission should be treated as useful information about the limits of the family's knowledge. Its description of established thread-wearing and ritual names is evidence of how the household understood its practice in 1927. Whether the practice began much earlier requires other evidence, but the account need not be reduced to an imitation of a recent movement. [1]

The Pals and the need to separate royal ancestry from local history

The connection to the Pala emperors remains unproved. A shared surname, an interpretation of Buddhist worship or a citation to an ancient ruler does not bridge the intervening centuries. The episode in which Radhakanta Pal rescues Kirtichand in 1743 also fails as literal biography if it refers to the Burdwan ruler who died in 1740. A remembered act of assistance may have acquired the wrong ruler's name, but that possible explanation is not itself corroboration. [1][11][12]

These problems are specific. They do not make every Pal detail false. The family text preserves named reservoirs, a shared sacrificial arrangement with Kesh households, businesses, marriages and numbered lawsuits. Each is capable of a different kind of verification. The Dharmamangal reading and the precise “second battle” wording also show why criticism of this account must attend closely to its sources. Even where a claim serves family prestige, deliberate invention must be demonstrated rather than presumed.

The Roys and the material landscape of Shanko

The Shanko narrative connects family memory to a fort, moats, tanks, sacred images and neighbouring settlements. These features could help distinguish successive phases of settlement and power. The account of human remains in a tank is not sufficient to identify a battle, but neither is it the only archaeologically testable claim: earthworks, buildings and water systems can also preserve evidence. Pratap Chandra Roy's publishing career belongs to a much better documented period and should be evaluated through its own records. [1]

The Chaudhuris and Hatis in a wider community

The Putsuri Chaudhuris' account is valuable for Suta identity, marriage, education and public service. Its manuscript story requires careful distinction between the copies and people involved. Sukumar Sen describes two extended Chaitanya Bhagavata witnesses associated with Denur and Kaigram. The presence of three additional chapters alone does not identify the Putsuri copy with either of them. Ambikacharan Chaudhuri, the family owner, must also be distinguished from Ambikacharan Brahmachari, the scholar who published additional chapters. The lineage entry is a lead to a manuscript, not proof of its present location. [1][14]

The Hati account offers comparatively little about remote origins and much about legal practice, education and public works. That is a strength for those questions, not a deficiency to be filled with someone else's ancestry. Read together, the Hati and Chaudhuri entries show how professional service and intermarriage helped sustain community connections alongside landholding and commerce. [1]

Tanks and temples record a claim to belonging

The repeated attention to water is one of the most persuasive patterns in the family histories. A tank served practical needs, but its patronage also placed a family within the village's memory. A reservoir associated with a wife or mother connected household remembrance to a public resource. A temple, a festival obligation or a lamp carried between villages could similarly make belonging visible and repeat it across generations. [1]

These acts help explain why family histories describe authority through things built, repaired and endowed. They record how a household expected its position to be remembered. The practical service and the claim to status could be present together. Recognising this does not require treating every number of tanks or every founding attribution as exact.

This evidence also has a social limit. The surviving narratives usually speak from the patron's side. The people who dug tanks, maintained fields or supplied unpaid labour seldom speak for themselves. A fuller community history would place those experiences beside the benefactor's account. The Sahana story of Ambika Devi insisting that tenants be well fed, for example, preserves both a remembered ethic of care and the unequal labour relationship within which that care operated. [1]

Ritual differences and a changing public identity

The Jana and Suta distinctions show that a shared community name did not mean uniform practice. Risley in 1891 records sacred-thread practice among Janas, but does not give the Jana twelve-day and Suta thirty-day mourning contrast. Vidyanidhi's 1896 discussion provides earlier testimony to Jana ritual claims, and the 1931 census explicitly records the twelve-day and thirty-day distinction. These sources locate debates about status and practice before, as well as during, the twentieth-century campaigns. They do not establish biological differences between the branches. [6][8][17]

The census also shows identity being negotiated in public. In 1921, some households returned themselves as Khatri; the reported decline in Aguri numbers therefore cannot be read simply as population loss. In 1931, the designation Ugra Kshattriya (Aguri) was accepted, and the report describes efforts to bring Jana and Suta practices together. It notes some shared dining among educated people but says that no instance of intermarriage had been reported before the census. This records a movement towards unity, not proof that a merger was already complete. The report did not separately tabulate the caste that year. [8]

The conclusion the evidence supports

The Aguris' clearest historical homeland is Rarh. Their recorded history joins cultivation and landholding with military service, trade and public life. The literary evidence, especially when early editions and manuscript variants are considered, favours an interpretation in which their regional history predates the eighteenth-century expansion of the Burdwan Raj. The 1903 Ramayana edition strengthens the possibility of a considerably earlier presence, while leaving the precise dating of its Agari passage to manuscript verification. [2][3][4][5][6][7][8][10]

The most convincing reconstruction is therefore a community with deep regional roots whose families did not necessarily share one origin. Mughal service may have introduced or strengthened some lineages; local houses may have joined larger military and revenue structures; marriage and ritual relationships could connect them over time. The Burdwan Raj gave important opportunities to an existing regional population and helped reshape its fortunes. This interpretation accommodates the strongest parts of the family traditions without making every remembered commander, number or royal descent literal history.

The name Ugra Kshatriya also has a history. It should not be treated simply as a label invented during the twentieth-century census campaigns: earlier printed sources and the literary passages discussed here already place Agari identity in relation to Kshatriya status. What became especially visible in the census era was organised debate over recognition, naming and relations between branches. The strength and spread of particular practices varied; an old household custom and a newer movement to extend it could exist together. [3][6][7][8][17]

What remains unestablished is a single biological ancestry: descent of all Aguris from Agra Rajputs, the Pala emperors, one named founder or one regiment. The absence of that proof does not leave the community without a history. Its history is already substantial in the landscapes it inhabited, the services its families performed, the relationships they maintained and the memories they passed on. A responsible community history can state that conclusion firmly while keeping particular ancestral questions open.
