How a new colonial revenue system created room for locally rooted Aguri landholders, moneylenders and grain dealers to become indispensable intermediaries between absentee patnidars and the villages they nominally owned.

Nineteenth-century descriptions of rural Burdwan often present a striking social landscape. At the top stood the Maharaja of Burdwan and the holders of large patni taluqs. Beneath them were substantial cultivators—especially Sadgops and Aguris—who controlled much of the land, lent grain and money, and exercised considerable influence over smaller peasants. The picture is familiar. What is less obvious is how these two worlds were connected.

A patni could be bought by a merchant, lawyer, court official or professional living in Calcutta, Uttarpara or another town far from the villages from which the rent had to be collected. Such an owner might hold legal rights over several villages without knowing which family cultivated which field, who was in debt, which rent-free claim was genuine, where an old boundary ran, or which village headman could mobilise resistance. The system could not function from an urban office alone. It required people on the ground.

This is where the dar-patni and the older village landed elite become crucial. Evidence from Burdwan suggests that locally powerful cultivators and headmen increasingly acquired subordinate permanent tenures—dar-patnis and se-patnis—under the superior patnidars. For prosperous Aguri families, who were already prominent as jotedars, grain dealers and moneylenders, this created a powerful route from cultivation into rent-receiving landed power.

From a Revenue Kingdom to a Revenue Machine

The change began before the Permanent Settlement. In 1760 Mir Qasim ceded the revenues of Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong to the East India Company. The Burdwan Raj remained the great landed power of the region, but the political meaning of that position was changing. Under the older order the Raj combined revenue collection with policing, military service, patronage, religious grants and a wide range of local obligations. Company rule gradually stripped away many of those functions while insisting ever more strictly on the punctual payment of cash revenue.

That distinction mattered enormously. Agricultural income was never perfectly predictable. Harvests varied, grain prices moved, cultivators delayed payment, villages resisted new assessments, and disputes could take months to settle. Yet the Company wanted a fixed sum on fixed dates. The Burdwan Raj therefore faced a structural problem: its obligation upward was rigid, while its collections downward remained flexible, negotiated and uncertain.

The Permanent Settlement of 1793 did not remove that problem. It fixed the Government demand, but it did not magically make the Raj's tenants pay on time. In January 1794, the Collector of Burdwan recorded the Raja's complaint that he was unable to realise rent from his tenants with the same punctuality with which he was required to remit revenue to Government. The later patni system grew directly out of this mismatch.

Patni: Passing the Revenue Risk Downward

The solution was ingenious. Instead of depending entirely on short-term revenue farmers, the Raj increasingly created permanent subordinate estates. A patni taluq was held at a fixed annual rent in perpetuity. The patnidar and his heirs received a transferable right to collect the rents of the villages included in the tenure. In return, the patnidar had to pay a fixed jama to the Burdwan Raj. If he defaulted, the tenure could be sold.

In practical terms, the arrangement shifted risk. The Maharaja no longer had to manage every village or bargain annually with every local renter. He could demand a predictable amount from the patnidar. The patnidar, in turn, had an incentive to collect more from the villages than he owed above.

The system developed gradually in the first years of the nineteenth century and was already widespread before the colonial Government gave it full legal recognition. Regulation VIII of 1819 formally declared patni tenures valid, hereditary and transferable. Equally important, it recognised the patnidar's right to create subordinate permanent leases. The hierarchy could therefore extend downward from patni to dar-patni, se-patni and still lower interests.

Who Were the Patnidars? An Important Qualification

At this point a distinction is essential. “Patnidar” was a legal and fiscal position, not the name of a caste. The surviving early examples that can be identified by name do not show Aguris dominating the first generation of superior patni purchasers. This does not mean that an Aguri could never be a patnidar. The distinction is chronological and structural. In the formative decades of the system, prosperous service families, merchants and other members of the wider landed gentry are prominent among the named superior tenure-holders. By the later nineteenth century, however, some successful Aguri families had accumulated enough capital to purchase patnis as well as dar-patnis. The most plausible trajectory for part of the Aguri elite was therefore not a fixed caste division between ‘patnidar’ and ‘Aguri’, but an upward movement from substantial jote and village credit, through subordinate tenureholding, and in some cases into superior patni ownership.

Dar-Patni: The Missing Middle

The dar-patni is the key to understanding how this system worked socially. A large patni owner did not necessarily collect rents directly. He could grant a perpetual under-tenure to another person, who would pay him a fixed annual amount and assume responsibility for collections below. This made the patni system attractive to people whose wealth or profession was centred outside the countryside.

By 1819 the Burdwan Raj contained 1,495 patni taluqs covering 8,738 villages. The smaller patnis were widely held by the local gentry, many of whom had experience in the older revenue-farming system. Beneath them, the number of dar-patnidars and still lower tenure holders continued to multiply. The result was not a simple landlord-and-peasant structure, but a ladder of rent-receiving rights.

Ratnalekha Ray's study of Burdwan provides a particularly revealing glimpse of what happened at the village end of that ladder. In 1820 the Collector of Burdwan complained that village mandals and headmen had begun taking their own villages in dar-patni or se-patni. His tone was hostile because he believed they could manipulate village accounts, but the underlying fact is more important: men who already possessed local authority were acquiring formal permanent rights inside the new tenure system.

This was not an entirely new social group. Before patni, principal village landholders had often taken the lowest level of temporary revenue farm, known as qutqina. Holding the farm helped them protect their own jotes and influence the assessment of the village. The dar-patni allowed the same strategy to continue in a new legal form. A locally dominant cultivator could remain a jotedar while also becoming a rent-receiving intermediary.

Why the Absentee Patnidar Needed Village Insiders

The need for such intermediaries becomes obvious once we look at estate management in practice. A patnidar living in Calcutta could own rights over villages many days away. He might know the annual jama, the purchase price of the tenure and the total amount expected from a local office. But those figures were only the top layer of a much more complicated rural economy.

Collection required intimate knowledge: which fields were actually cultivated, which families claimed hereditary occupancy, which plots had been treated as rent-free, who had borrowed grain, who could pay after harvest, which tenants had absconded, and which village headman could persuade others to resist an enhancement. Much of this information was customary and local. It could not simply be read from a clean ledger in Calcutta.

The experience of Jaykrishna Mukherjee of Uttarpara illustrates the problem. His family accumulated landed interests across several districts. Jaykrishna was unusually determined to retain direct managerial control, yet even he depended on managers, clerks, gomastas and local agents. His biographer describes a rural world in which the mandal, the mahajan and the gomasta formed a practical triangle of power. The mandal knew the village and its records. The mahajan controlled credit. The gomasta represented the landlord. If these men cooperated, they could make an absentee estate function; if they colluded against the owner, they could also frustrate him.

Jagabandhu Hazra Chowdhury is recorded as having mediated a settlement between the patnidar Jaykrishna Mukhopadhyay and local cultivators resisting his rent demands. The account shows negotiation alongside conflict in rural rent relations, with locally influential figures helping to bring disputing parties together.[source]

Most landlords therefore had two choices. They could build an expensive bureaucracy of trusted agents and supervise it closely, or they could pass the problem downward by creating under-tenures. Dar-patni was, in this sense, not merely another legal complication. It was a technology of absentee management.

Why Aguri Jotedars Were Well Placed to Fill the Gap

This is where the Aguris enter the story most clearly. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century sources repeatedly describe the Aguris, or Ugra Kshatriyas, as one of the most influential cultivating communities of Burdwan and the neighbouring Rarh districts. They were not uniformly wealthy—there were middle and poor Aguri cultivators as well—but a conspicuous upper stratum held large jotes, traded in grain, lent money and exercised considerable local influence.

These activities gave prosperous Aguri families exactly the assets an absentee patnidar needed: land, local information, working capital and social authority. A large jotedar knew the productive capacity of the village because he cultivated or supervised land himself. A grain dealer knew the harvest and the market. A moneylender knew which households were solvent and which were already in debt. A mandal or other village notable knew the customary hierarchy of the settlement.

Credit was especially important. Rent dates did not always coincide with the moment when a cultivator had cash in hand. A local mahajan could advance money or grain, enable the rent to be paid, and recover the advance after harvest with interest. The same person might purchase the crop, hold a mortgage, or acquire an under-tenure. Landholding, rent collection, grain trading and moneylending therefore reinforced one another.

For a prosperous Aguri household, the step from jotedar to dar-patnidar was consequently not as large as it may first appear. The family was already embedded in the village economy. Acquiring the under-tenure converted informal influence into a formal right to receive rents.

From Jote to Dar-Patni: A Pathway of Upward Mobility

The emergence of the Aguri landed elite is best understood as a process rather than a single historical event. In the eighteenth century, locally important cultivators could accumulate large holdings, act as headmen, lend grain or money, and sometimes take temporary revenue farms. After the Permanent Settlement and the spread of patni, those same kinds of families could acquire permanent subordinate rights.

The sequence might look like this: a family first built a substantial jote; agricultural surplus supported grain trading and lending; credit relationships increased control over smaller cultivators; village office or local reputation strengthened bargaining power; and a dar-patni or se-patni then converted that local power into a heritable rent-receiving asset. In the most successful cases, profits could be reinvested in additional land, larger patnis, commerce, rice mills, coal and mica ventures, education and urban professions.

This helps explain why the nineteenth-century Aguri elite could appear simultaneously as cultivators, landlords, traders and creditors. These were not separate identities. They were different parts of the same process of capital accumulation.

The Families Who Moved Up the Ladder

Achintya Kumar Dutta's work on the Ugra Kshatriyas provides some of the clearest family-level examples. He identifies the Roy Chowdhurys of Chaitanyapur, the Tahs of Palitpur, the Hazras of Anguna and the Panjas of Sathinandi among prosperous Aguri families that acquired patni and dar-patni interests, either from the Burdwan Raj or from the Mukherjees of Uttarpara.

These families show how the system could operate in more than one direction. Urban or larger landed houses such as the Uttarpara Mukherjees could hold superior estates, while locally rooted families acquired the subordinate tenure and handled the village economy. Over time, some of those subordinate families accumulated enough capital to acquire superior interests of their own.

The Hazras of Anguna later combined landed wealth with rice milling and coal mining. The Panjas of Sathinandi built fortunes through commission trade in grain, oilseeds, poppy seed and tobacco and reinvested profits in agricultural land. In Bankura, the Sahana family combined cultivation, moneylending, grain dealing, rent collection, mining and rice milling on a still larger scale. By the early twentieth century Satya Kinkar Sahana was described as a moneylending landlord and aratdar with several zamindari interests. These later careers illuminate the long-term logic of the earlier system: rural surplus could be converted into commercial capital, and commercial capital could flow back into land.

The distinction matters. The Aguri story is strongest at the level of the resident landed intermediary: the jotedar, creditor, grain dealer, mandal and under-tenure holder who knew the village economy from within. Some of those families later climbed higher. Thus, saying simply that ‘Aguris were not patnidars’ would be too absolute; saying that they were not the clearly identifiable core of the first documented superior patnidar group, while later Aguri elites entered both dar-patni and patni ownership, fits the evidence much better.

Sonapalasi: Looking Again at the Village Hierarchy

Tara Krishna Basu's well-known description of Sonapalasi, the village associated with Lal Behari Day, captures the social result of this process. He described a village in which the superior zamindar held a patni under the Maharaja of Burdwan, while large Sadgop and Aguri peasants formed the economic backbone of the settlement. Their moneylending and grain-lending businesses gave them influence over smaller cultivators.

Read literally, the passage seems to place the patnidar and the Aguri jotedar in two separate social compartments. The wider evidence suggests a more fluid reality. A prosperous Aguri could be a large cultivator and a creditor while also holding a dar-patni or another subordinate rent-receiving right. Even when he did not, his command of land and credit made him indispensable to the superior landlord.

The real hierarchy of a Burdwan village could therefore run from the Maharaja to the patnidar, from the patnidar to a dar-patnidar or se-patnidar, and from there to mandals, jotedars, mahajans and ordinary raiyats. The same family could occupy more than one of these middle positions at the same time.

A New Way to Understand Aguri Dominance

This interpretation changes the way we understand the rise of the Aguri landed class. One traditional explanation links Aguri prosperity to an earlier military role under the Burdwan Raj, especially the Nagdi or cash-paid troops. Later Ugra Kshatriya writings often remembered a martial past, and some modern historians have repeated the claim that disbanded Aguri soldiers moved into agriculture.

The military connection should be treated with care. Surviving eighteenth-century troop returns confirm that the Burdwan Raj maintained substantial Nagdi forces, but the available returns do not provide a caste breakdown that would allow the entire establishment to be identified as Aguri. A military background may well have mattered for particular families, but it is not necessary to explain the later rise of the Aguri rural elite.

The agrarian mechanism is much better documented. Aguris were already prominent among the substantial cultivators of the region. The colonial revenue order created a market in rent-receiving rights. Patni opened the upper level; dar-patni and se-patni opened the levels closer to the village. Families that already possessed land, cash, grain stocks, credit networks and local authority were best placed to take advantage of those opportunities.

The Long-Term Consequence: Power Without Owning the Top Estate

One of the most important lessons of the Burdwan story is that rural power did not require ownership of the highest estate. A family could exercise enormous influence from the middle of the hierarchy. The superior patnidar might possess the title recognised in the larger legal and revenue system, but the resident intermediary often possessed the knowledge, credit and social relationships that made the title economically useful.

This helps explain how a relatively small regional community such as the Aguris could become so conspicuous in the economic life of Burdwan. Their influence rested not simply on caste status or on one occupation. It rested on a combination of productive land, control over credit and grain, rent-receiving interests, village authority and, later, commercial diversification.

The patni system therefore did more than protect the Burdwan Raj from the pressure of Company revenue demands. It rearranged the structure of opportunity below the Raj. Large urban investors could acquire secure rent-bearing property, but they still needed people who understood the villages. Dar-patni created a legal place for those people inside the system.

For prosperous Aguri jotedars, that place could become a ladder. A family might begin as a substantial cultivator, become a lender and grain dealer, acquire an under-tenure, collect rents from neighbours, purchase further land, and eventually enter the ranks of local landlords and commercial entrepreneurs. The nineteenth-century Aguri landed class was therefore not simply a survival of an older social order. It was also a product of the new one.

Patni solved the Burdwan Raj's fiscal problem. Dar-patni solved the absentee landlord's management problem. Locally rooted Aguri landholders were among the families well placed to turn that middle position into lasting rural power.
