Bardhaman and its neighbouring districts form the historical heartland of Aguri community life. Their villages, river valleys and changing towns connect family histories with the wider story of Rarh Bengal.

Start with a village, a school or a family connection. The map above gives regional bearings; the stories below show what particular records can tell us about life in those places. Old records may call Bardhaman Burdwan or Bardwan: use the place-name guide when following a reference.

Read the overview and its evidence

Aguri history belongs to a broad agricultural landscape shaped by the Ajay–Kunur, Damodar and eastern Bardhaman’s smaller waterways. Village studies at Kshiragram and in Raina document substantial Aguri landholding, while family and commercial histories connect cultivation with grain trading, rice milling and investment in land.[cite:togawa-2006][cite:ruud-2003][cite:dutta-2023]

The principal modern canal schemes reached a countryside where these communities were already established. Irrigation could change agricultural opportunity, but its effects depended on landownership, access to water and local conditions; the evidence does not show that every Aguri household benefited alike.

The article follows this history from river plains to canal command areas, distinguishing documented village histories from proposed connections to particular river basins.

The regional heartland

Reference accounts locate the Aguri community particularly in Bardhaman, Birbhum, Bankura and Hooghly. These are broad historical associations, not exclusive boundaries or a census of today’s population.[cite:aguri-reference] An anthropological study of Rarh, drawing on the 1931 census, likewise places agriculturist castes “such as Sadgop, Goala, and Aguri” in undivided Bardhaman and in some parts of Birbhum (Mukhopadhyay 2026, p. 7).[cite:mukhopadhyay-2026]

Reading the map: The rivers and towns above provide regional bearings. For village histories, follow the place-specific accounts and sources below; rivers and district lines do not define the community’s extent.[cite:purba-geography]

More than red earth

Rarh is often evoked through red earth, but the landscape also includes river valleys and fertile alluvial plains. The official account of Purba Bardhaman places it principally between the Ajay, Bhagirathi–Hooghly and Damodar rivers and describes the variety of its landforms.[cite:purba-geography]

The western district’s historical account associates Gopbhum with the land between the Ajay and Damodar. Places such as these provide context for the competing histories of regional power and community identity.[cite:paschim-history]

These are historical enumerations under the geographical and classificatory conventions of their sources. They are not present-day population estimates, and changes between rows should not be read as a simple demographic trend without examining those conventions.

As an approximate comparison, the official census table gives Barddhaman district totals of 1,533,868 in 1911 and 1,434,766 in 1921, adjusted to 2011 boundaries. Set against Dutta’s Aguri counts of 66,551 and 59,949, these give about 4.3 and 4.2 per cent. Dutta’s counts follow the boundaries of their time, so the comparison is not exact.[cite:census-a2-west-bengal][cite:dutta-2023]

Ruud reports a different district share of 7.6 per cent for 1921, alongside 11.1 per cent for Raina in 1901 (p. 123).[cite:ruud-2003] The reported 7.6 per cent does not match the calculation above; its underlying denominator and boundaries still need checking. Raina and the district also differ in area and year. These figures should not be combined into one community-wide share, and none is a present-day estimate.

Following families across places

Dutta’s examples connect specific families and villages: the Hazras of Anguna in the trans-Damodar region, the Panjas of Sathinandi, the Tahs of Palitpur, the Roy Chowdhurys of Chaitanyapur and the Sahana family of Bankura (pp. 308–310).[cite:dutta-2023] Read about family enterprise and landholding.

Village fieldwork adds to this picture. At Kshiragram, in the Mangalkot block, Masahiko Togawa counted Jana and Suta Aguri households and title-defined lineages; at Musharu, Salini Saha found Ugra Kshatriyas the dominant farming community.[cite:togawa-2006][cite:saha-2026] In Raina I block, south of the Damodar, Arild Engelsen Ruud studied a village he calls Gopinathpur, where Aguris were the dominant landholding caste in 1993; he disguised its name, so it cannot be placed on a map (pp. 16–17).[cite:ruud-2003]

The villages were linked to one another. Each month the Kshiragram temple calls the Aguris of Eruyar, Nasigram, Kusumgram, Kurmun, Kaligram and Majigram, which Togawa describes as Ugra Kshatriya-dominated villages around the Mangalkot area (pp. 44, 86). A Raychaudhuri lineage chart reported by Togawa traces one family from pargana Dhenya to Eruyar in about 1746–47, to Kshiragram in about 1774–75 and to Chaitanyapur in about 1794–95, an example of families moving between neighbouring villages (pp. 148–149).[cite:togawa-2006]

Village records, dated family documents and contributed oral histories could make the geographical story more precise. A place index should distinguish where someone was born, lived, held land, worked or published; those locations are not necessarily the same.
