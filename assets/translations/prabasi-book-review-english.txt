EDITORIAL CONTEXT

This translation covers the complete review at the foot of printed p. 470 and the top of p. 471, excluding the unrelated reviews around it. The reviewer is describing the book’s origin theory; that description is not proof of the theory. It concerns an earlier edition, not the 1940 revision.



TRANSLATOR’S NOTES

1. The compiler’s surname in this review is read as Basu (বসু); the 1940 book’s title leaf names Haricharan Bandhu (বন্ধু). This discrepancy is preserved rather than silently harmonised.

2. A short lineage description following Agnikula Rajput on p. 470 is unclear in the scan and is marked in the translation.

3. The Sanskrit quotation is attributed in the review to the Brihaddharma Purana. Its use here belongs to the reviewer’s historical argument about caste status.

4. The review’s puzzled comment on an English foreword belongs to the earlier edition.

5. The Bengali transcription was checked against the two scans. Printed line breaks are joined; square brackets mark an unreadable phrase, and the dash marks the sentence continuing onto the next page.



The book and its argument

Printed p. 470 · scan 498 · lower right column

Source image: assets/books/prabasi-498.jpg



Rajput O Ugrakshatriya. Compiled and edited by Sri Haricharan Basu [surname as read in this review]. Publisher: Sri Ashutosh Chowdhury, Burdwan. No price is stated. The origins, customs, practices, observances, lineage conventions and social standing of the Ugra Kshatriya community have been collected in this book from various scriptures and from regional literature and history.

The author has sought to show that the Vedic Agnikula Rajputs [a following lineage description is unclear in the scan] came to Bengal as soldiers of the Muslim conquerors, established settlements in Burdwan district, and came to be known as Ugra Kshatriyas. Later, during Akbar’s reign, Raja Man Singh’s Kshatriya—



The reviewer’s assessment

Printed p. 471 · scan 499 · upper left column

Source image: assets/books/prabasi-499.jpg



—soldiers also came to live in that part to assist the governor of Burdwan. The lineage born of the union of these two groups of Kshatriya settlers is identified through the Brihaddharma Purana’s statement: “Ugra and Rajputra were born of a Kshatriya by her (a Vaishya woman).” Therefore, they are Kshatriyas.

Although this book deals with the history of one particular community, it seems likely to be enjoyable reading for readers interested in the study of communities. We do not understand why the introduction to this book was written in English.
